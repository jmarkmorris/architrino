### WGC
- The **Weak Gravity Conjecture (WGC)** is a theoretical idea in the realm of quantum gravity. 
-  This conjecture was first proposed by Nima Arkani-Hamed, Luboš Motl, Alberto Nicolis, and Cumrun Vafa in 2007.
- It suggests that in any consistent theory of quantum gravity, gravity should be the weakest force compared to other gauge forces.
- This means that for some particles, the force mediated by other interactions (like electromagnetism) should be stronger than the gravitational force.
- The WGC has significant implications for our understanding of black holes, particle physics, and the structure of the universe. It's a fascinating area of study that bridges many different fields of physics and mathematics.
- Quantum theories of fundamental forces describe how particles interact via force-carrying particles (like photons for electromagnetism). 
- In a consistent theory of quantum gravity, it’s essential to see how these forces balance out, especially at very high energies.
- The WGC posits that, for any particle in nature, there should be a counterpart particle with a stronger non-gravitational force than gravitational force. 
- This essentially means that for charged particles, electromagnetic interactions should dominate over gravitational interactions.
- **Theoretical Implications**: One motivation behind the WGC is to ensure the stability of black holes. 
- According to the conjecture, there are particles that prevent black holes from decaying in a way that's inconsistent with quantum mechanics and general relativity. 
- If gravity were not the weakest force, it could lead to violations of these principles.
- **Why It Matters**: The WGC links the strength of forces to deeper properties of space, time, and matter. It plays a role in string theory and the quest for a unified theory of all fundamental interactions, providing a constraint that must be satisfied for any viable theory of quantum gravity.
- While gravity is the weakest force, the WGC ensures that in a consistent quantum gravitational framework, gravity's weakness is not just an observed fact but a necessary feature of the universe's structure. 

### Thermal Freeze out
- **Thermal freeze-out** is a concept in particle physics and cosmology that describes how certain particles in the early universe became relics as the universe expanded and cooled. Here's a breakdown:
- 1. **Early Universe Conditions**: In the hot, dense early universe, particles were in thermal equilibrium, meaning they interacted frequently and maintained a stable distribution.
- 2. **Expansion and Cooling**: As the universe expanded, it cooled down, and the rate of interactions between particles decreased.
- 3. **Decoupling**: When the rate of expansion (described by the Hubble parameter, H) became greater than the rate of particle interactions, particles "froze out" of thermal equilibrium. This means they stopped interacting significantly with other particles.
- 4. **Relic Abundance**: The particles that froze out remained in the universe, leading to a relic abundance. This is particularly important for understanding dark matter, as it's believed that weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) froze out and now make up dark matter.
- In essence, thermal freeze-out explains how particles that were once abundant and interacting in the early universe became relics that we can observe today, such as dark matter.
- Relic Particles
	- **Relic particles** are particles from the early universe that have survived to the present day. They were created during the first moments after the Big Bang when the universe was extremely hot and dense. As the universe expanded and cooled, these particles "froze out" of thermal equilibrium and stopped interacting frequently with other particles. Because they no longer annihilated or decayed, they became "relics" of the early universe.
	- 1. **Photons of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)**: These are the oldest light in the universe, dating back to about 380,000 years after the Big Bang when atoms first formed, and the universe became transparent.
	- 2. **Neutrinos**: Neutrinos are incredibly light, weakly interacting particles that decoupled from the hot plasma of the early universe just one second after the Big Bang.
	- 3. **Dark Matter Particles**: Many theories suggest that dark matter consists of relic particles, such as Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs), that froze out early in the universe's history.
	- 4. **Primordial Black Holes**: Although not particles in the traditional sense, these black holes could have formed from density fluctuations in the very early universe and persisted to the present day as relics.
	- These relic particles are important because they carry information about the conditions and processes in the early universe, helping scientists understand the history and evolution of the cosmos.
