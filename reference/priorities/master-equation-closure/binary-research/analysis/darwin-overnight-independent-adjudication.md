## 1. The pair law in centre and relative coordinates

Let the members be $1,2$ with positions $\mathbf X_1,\mathbf X_2\in\mathbb R^3$, velocities $\mathbf V_i=d\mathbf X_i/dT$, separation vector $\mathbf r=\mathbf X_1-\mathbf X_2$, $r=\|\mathbf r\|$, $\mathbf e=\mathbf r/r$, polarity sign $\sigma=\pm1$ ($\sigma=-1$ opposite polarity, $\sigma=+1$ same polarity), $K=c_f=1$. Define the symmetric $3\times3$ coupling matrix

$$
M(\mathbf r)=\frac{\sigma}{2r}\left(I+\mathbf e\mathbf e^{T}\right),
$$

so that the frozen functional reads $\mathcal L=\tfrac12\|\mathbf V_1\|^2+\tfrac12\|\mathbf V_2\|^2+\mathbf V_1^{T}M\mathbf V_2-\sigma/r$. $M$ has eigenvalue $\sigma/r$ along $\mathbf e$ and $\sigma/(2r)$ on the two transverse directions. Introduce the centre and relative variables $\mathbf R=\tfrac12(\mathbf X_1+\mathbf X_2)$, $\mathbf U=\dot{\mathbf R}=\tfrac12(\mathbf V_1+\mathbf V_2)$, $\mathbf u=\dot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf V_1-\mathbf V_2$. Because $M$ is symmetric the cross terms cancel and

$$
\mathcal L=\mathbf U^{T}\left(I+M(\mathbf r)\right)\mathbf U+\tfrac14\,\mathbf u^{T}\left(I-M(\mathbf r)\right)\mathbf u-\frac{\sigma}{r}.
$$

This is exact, not an approximation, and it is the whole route: the velocity-quadratic part splits into a common-centre block with weight $I+M$ and a relative block with weight $I-M$, coupled only through the dependence of $M$ on $\mathbf r$. The Euler–Lagrange equations are

$$
\frac{d}{dT}\Big[2\,(I+M)\,\mathbf U\Big]=\mathbf 0,\qquad
\frac{d}{dT}\Big[\tfrac12\,(I-M)\,\mathbf u\Big]=\nabla_{\mathbf r}\Big[\tfrac14\mathbf u^{T}(I-M)\mathbf u-\frac{\sigma}{r}\Big]+\nabla_{\mathbf r}\Big[\mathbf U^{T}M\mathbf U\Big],
$$

where $\nabla_{\mathbf r}M$ acts on the explicit $\mathbf r$ dependence. In member coordinates these are the same equations as $\dot{\mathbf p}_i=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf X_i$ with $\mathbf p_1=\mathbf V_1+M\mathbf V_2$, $\mathbf p_2=\mathbf V_2+M\mathbf V_1$; the change of variables is linear and invertible, so nothing is lost. Both polarities are covered by the sign $\sigma$ inside $M$ and the pair term; their singular loci are kept separate below.

### 1.1 The 6×6 velocity Hessian and its exact invertibility domain

In member velocities the Hessian is $H=\begin{pmatrix}I&M\\M&I\end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvectors are $(\mathbf w,\mathbf w)$ with eigenvalue $1+\mu$ and $(\mathbf w,-\mathbf w)$ with eigenvalue $1-\mu$, where $\mu$ runs over the eigenvalues of $M$ with eigenvector $\mathbf w$. The complete spectrum, with multiplicities, is

$$
1+\frac{\sigma}{r}\ (\text{sum radial}),\quad 1+\frac{\sigma}{2r}\ (\text{sum transverse},\times2),\quad 1-\frac{\sigma}{r}\ (\text{difference radial}),\quad 1-\frac{\sigma}{2r}\ (\text{difference transverse},\times2).
$$

Sum modes $(\mathbf w,\mathbf w)$ are common-centre modes ($\mathbf U$); difference modes $(\mathbf w,-\mathbf w)$ are mirror modes ($\mathbf u$). $\det H=(1-1/r^2)(1-1/(4r^2))^2$ for either polarity, so $H$ is singular exactly at $r=1$ and $r=\tfrac12$ (units $K/c_f^2$) and invertible for every other $r>0$. Which modes are singular depends on polarity:

- Opposite polarity ($\sigma=-1$): singular at $r=1$ in the sum radial mode $(\mathbf e,\mathbf e)$ and at $r=\tfrac12$ in the two sum transverse modes. The three difference (mirror) eigenvalues $1+1/r$, $1+1/(2r)$ are positive for all $r>0$. $H$ is positive definite for $r>1$, has one negative eigenvalue for $\tfrac12<r<1$, three for $r<\tfrac12$.
- Same polarity ($\sigma=+1$): singular at $r=1$ in the difference radial mode $(\mathbf e,-\mathbf e)$ and at $r=\tfrac12$ in the two difference transverse modes. The three sum eigenvalues are positive for all $r>0$. Positive definite for $r>1$; indefinite below.

Grade: derived (exact algebra; the receipt prints the four eigenvalues at every benchmark separation under `hessianEigs`).

### 1.2 Zero velocity does not remove the coupling from the acceleration solve

At $\mathbf V_1=\mathbf V_2=\mathbf 0$ every velocity-quadratic term in $\dot{\mathbf p}_i$ and in $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf X_i$ vanishes, leaving $H\mathbf A=\frac{\sigma}{r^2}(\mathbf e,-\mathbf e)$. The right side is a pure difference radial vector, so

$$
\mathbf A_1=-\mathbf A_2=\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma/r}\,\frac{\mathbf e}{r^2}=\frac{\sigma\,\mathbf e}{r\,(r-\sigma)}.
$$

The factor $(1-\sigma/r)^{-1}$ is the velocity coupling acting through the Hessian at zero velocity. For opposite polarity the rest acceleration magnitude is $1/(r(r+1))$, weaker than the inverse-square value $1/r^2$; for same polarity it is $1/(r(r-1))$, stronger, and it diverges at the same-polarity singular separation $r=1$. The manuscript's statement that zero velocity does not remove the coupling is confirmed exactly, and the coupling is a multiplicative factor on the rest acceleration, not an additive correction. Grade: derived.

## 2. Invariants and symmetries

The functional has no explicit $T$ dependence, is invariant under common translation $\mathbf X_i\to\mathbf X_i+\mathbf c$ (only $\mathbf r$ enters) and under common rotation $\mathbf X_i\to Q\mathbf X_i$, $\mathbf V_i\to Q\mathbf V_i$ (every term is a scalar built from $\mathbf V_i$, $\mathbf e$, $r$). Noether's procedure applied to the Euler–Lagrange line gives three exact invariants of this adapted law; none is a physical energy, momentum or angular-momentum account, and the quadratic velocity term is a unit-weight normalization, not a mass.

$$
E=\tfrac12\|\mathbf V_1\|^2+\tfrac12\|\mathbf V_2\|^2+\mathbf V_1^{T}M\mathbf V_2+\frac{\sigma}{r}
=\mathbf U^{T}(I+M)\mathbf U+\tfrac14\mathbf u^{T}(I-M)\mathbf u+\frac{\sigma}{r},
$$

$$
\mathbf P=\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2=2\,(I+M(\mathbf r))\,\mathbf U,\qquad
\mathbf J=\mathbf X_1\times\mathbf p_1+\mathbf X_2\times\mathbf p_2=\mathbf R\times\mathbf P+\mathbf r\times\tfrac12(I-M)\mathbf u .
$$

The energy-like function is the Jacobi function $\sum\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf p_i-\mathcal L$, which equals the velocity-quadratic part plus $\sigma/r$ because that part is homogeneous of degree two. The generalized momentum sum $\mathbf P$ is not $2\mathbf U$: the centre velocity $\mathbf U$ is not constant along a history unless $\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$ or $M$ is constant. A Galilean boost $\mathbf V_i\to\mathbf V_i+\mathbf w$ changes $\mathcal L$ by $\mathbf w^{T}M(\mathbf r)\,(\mathbf V_1+\mathbf V_2)+\mathbf w^{T}M(\mathbf r)\mathbf w$ plus a total derivative; the first two terms depend on $\mathbf r$ and are not total time derivatives, so the boost is not a symmetry and there is no boost invariant. The law singles out the frame in which it is written, which is consistent with the absolute time and Euclidean void of the substrate but means that a drifting pair is not the rest pair in motion. Time reversal $T\to-T$ and the discrete map $\Phi:(\mathbf X_1,\mathbf X_2)\mapsto(-\mathbf X_2,-\mathbf X_1)$ (inversion composed with relabeling) are symmetries since $\mathcal L$ is even in velocities and symmetric in the two members. Grade: derived.

## 3. Mirror preparations stay mirror; the reduced planar problem

Theorem (mirror invariance). Let $(\mathbf X_1,\mathbf X_2)(T)$ be a history of the full pair equations on $[0,T_1)$ along which $H$ is invertible, with $\mathbf X_1(0)=-\mathbf X_2(0)$ and $\mathbf V_1(0)=-\mathbf V_2(0)$. Then $\mathbf X_1(T)=-\mathbf X_2(T)$ on $[0,T_1)$.

Proof. Where $H$ is invertible the Euler–Lagrange line is an explicit second-order system $\mathbf A=H^{-1}\mathbf b(\mathbf X,\mathbf V)$ with $\mathbf b$ smooth for $r>0$, so solutions are unique. $\Phi$ maps histories to histories (it preserves $r$, $\mathbf e\mathbf e^{T}$, both velocity contractions and the sum structure), and the mirror initial data are a fixed point of $\Phi$. The history and its $\Phi$-image therefore share initial data and coincide. A second proof, needing only the common-centre block: mirror data give $\mathbf P=\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf P$ is conserved, and $(I+M)\mathbf U=\mathbf 0$ forces $\mathbf U=\mathbf 0$ wherever $I+M$ is invertible, so $\mathbf R$ stays at the origin. Hypothesis needed: invertibility of $H$ (first proof) or of $I+M$ (second proof) along the history. For opposite polarity $I+M$ is singular at $r=1$ and $r=\tfrac12$; for same polarity never. Under the frozen rule a singular $H$ ends the history with an obstruction event, so the hypothesis holds on every admitted history by construction. Grade: derived.

On the mirror manifold the $\mathbf r$ equation loses the term $\nabla_{\mathbf r}(\mathbf U^{T}M\mathbf U)$, which is quadratic in $\mathbf U$, so the reduced dynamics is the Euler–Lagrange dynamics of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{red}}=\tfrac14\mathbf u^{T}(I-M)\mathbf u-\sigma/r$. Rotational symmetry gives the conserved reduced angular momentum $\boldsymbol\ell=\mathbf r\times\tfrac12(I-M)\mathbf u=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\tfrac{\sigma}{2r}\bigr)\mathbf r\times\mathbf u$, which is normal to $\mathbf r$, so the motion stays in a fixed plane (or on a line when $\boldsymbol\ell=\mathbf 0$). With plane polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ for $\mathbf r$ and the abbreviations

$$
a(r)=1-\frac{\sigma}{r},\qquad b(r)=1-\frac{\sigma}{2r},\qquad h(r)=b(r)\,r^2=r^2-\frac{\sigma r}{2},
$$

the reduced Lagrangian, angular momentum, energy-like function and effective potential are

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{red}}=\tfrac14 a(r)\dot r^2+\tfrac14 h(r)\dot\theta^2-\frac{\sigma}{r},\qquad
\ell=\tfrac12 h(r)\dot\theta,\qquad
E=\tfrac14 a(r)\dot r^2+W(r),\qquad
W(r)=\frac{\ell^2}{h(r)}+\frac{\sigma}{r}.
$$

$a$ and $b$ are exactly the difference-mode Hessian eigenvalues of Section 1.1, so the reduced problem is regular wherever the mirror modes are regular: everywhere for opposite polarity, and for $r\notin\{1,\tfrac12\}$ for same polarity. The individual speed of either member is $\|\mathbf V_1\|=\tfrac12\|\mathbf u\|=\tfrac12\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2}$. The radial quadrature is $\dot r^2=4\,(E-W(r))/a(r)$.

### 3.1 Exact circular balance

The radial Euler–Lagrange equation on $\dot r=0$ reduces to $\tfrac14 h'(r_0)\dot\theta^2+\sigma/r_0^2=0$, so

$$
\omega^2=\frac{-4\sigma}{r_0^2\,h'(r_0)}=\frac{-8\sigma}{r_0^2\,(4r_0-\sigma)}.
$$

Opposite polarity ($\sigma=-1$), for every $r_0>0$:

$$
\omega^2=\frac{8}{r_0^2(4r_0+1)},\quad
v^2=\frac{r_0^2\omega^2}{4}=\frac{2}{4r_0+1},\quad
\ell^2=\frac{(2r_0+1)^2}{2(4r_0+1)},\quad
E=-\frac{2}{4r_0+1},\quad
\text{period}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}.
$$

Compared with the zero-coupling values $\omega^2=2/r_0^3$, $v^2=1/(2r_0)$, $E=-1/(2r_0)$, every quantity carries the factor $4r_0/(4r_0+1)$ or its square root: the circle exists at every separation but turns more slowly. The individual speed is below $c_f$ exactly when $r_0>\tfrac14$ and equals $c_f$ at $r_0=\tfrac14$; the circles with $r_0\le1$ sit where the full Hessian is indefinite or singular. Small radial oscillations about the circle: from $\tfrac14 a\dot r^2+W(r)=E$ at fixed $\ell$, $\tfrac12 a(r_0)\ddot{\delta r}+W''(r_0)\,\delta r=0$, and the identity $W''(r_0)=8/(r_0(4r_0+1)(2r_0+1))$ (obtained by eliminating $\ell^2$ with the balance relation; the receipt evaluates $W''$ numerically) gives

$$
\kappa^2=\frac{2W''(r_0)}{a(r_0)}=\frac{16}{(4r_0+1)(2r_0+1)(r_0+1)},\qquad
\frac{\kappa^2}{\omega^2}=\frac{2r_0^2}{(2r_0+1)(r_0+1)}<1,
$$

so the radial period exceeds the orbital period and the apsides advance by $\Delta\psi=2\pi\bigl(\sqrt{(2r_0+1)(r_0+1)/(2r_0^2)}-1\bigr)\approx 3\pi/(2r_0)$ per radial period. Since $W''>0$ and $a>0$ for all $r_0>0$, every opposite-polarity circle is linearly stable within the reduced planar problem, including those below $r_0=1$ where the full Hessian is indefinite; that qualification matters and is repeated in Section 7.

Same polarity ($\sigma=+1$): $\omega^2=-8/(r_0^2(4r_0-1))$ is positive only for $r_0<\tfrac14$, and there $v^2=2/(1-4r_0)>2$. No same-polarity circle exists at any $r_0\ge\tfrac14$; the formal circles below $\tfrac14$ lie beyond both same-polarity singular separations, have both mirror Hessian eigenvalues negative, and have individual speed above $\sqrt2\,c_f$, outside every speed label. Within the declared domain there is no same-polarity circle. Grade: derived.

## 5. Closed forms for the radial legs of the frozen law

For $\ell=0$ the radial quadrature $\dot r^2=4(E-\sigma/r)/a(r)$ integrates in elementary functions because $a(r)\,r=r-\sigma$. With turning point $r_t=\sigma/E$:

- opposite polarity ($E<0$, $r<r_t$): $t(r_{\mathrm{lo}}\to r_{\mathrm{hi}})=\tfrac{\sqrt{r_t}}2\,[F_L(r_{\mathrm{hi}}+1)-F_L(r_{\mathrm{lo}}+1)]$, $F_L(x)=L\arcsin\sqrt{x/L}-\sqrt{x(L-x)}$, $L=r_t+1$; individual speed $v(r)=\sqrt{(r_t-r)/(r_t(r+1))}$;
- same polarity ($E>0$, $r>r_t$): $t=\tfrac{\sqrt{r_t}}2\,[G_{L'}(r_{\mathrm{hi}}-1)-G_{L'}(r_{\mathrm{lo}}-1)]$, $G_{L'}(x)=\sqrt{x(x-L')}+L'\ln(\sqrt x+\sqrt{x-L'})$, $L'=r_t-1$; $v(r)=\sqrt{(r-r_t)/(r_t(r-1))}$.

## 7. Adversarial checks, limits and falsifiers

### 7.1 What the mirror reduction never exercises

The reduction lives entirely in the difference block of $H$. For opposite polarity the two singular separations $r=1$ and $r=\tfrac12$ both belong to sum (common-centre) modes, which the reduced problem never touches; a common-centre perturbation $\mathbf U\neq\mathbf 0$ is the only way to probe them, and an out-of-plane perturbation of $\mathbf r$ alone merely tilts the plane by rotational symmetry. For same polarity the singular separations are mirror modes, so the reduced problem meets them directly: $a(r)=1-1/r$ vanishes at $r=1$, the radial equation degenerates, and the reduced same-polarity problem is itself obstructed there. Nothing in the benchmark list reaches $r=1$ with same polarity ($r_{\min}=80.16$ in 6.5).

### 7.2 Linearization of the full equations about the exact circle

The common-centre equation is exactly $\frac{d}{dT}[(I+M(\mathbf r))\mathbf U]=\mathbf 0$ and the only back-reaction on $\mathbf r$ is $\nabla_{\mathbf r}(\mathbf U^{T}M\mathbf U)$, quadratic in $\mathbf U$. The twelve-dimensional linearization about the circle is therefore block triangular: the relative in-plane block (four dimensions) has exponents $\pm i\kappa$ and a double zero (phase shift and the neighbouring-circle direction); the relative out-of-plane block (two dimensions) has $\pm i\omega$ because a tilted circle is again a solution; the common block (six dimensions) is $\dot{\mathbf R}=\tfrac12(I+M(\mathbf r_0(T)))^{-1}\mathbf P$, $\dot{\mathbf P}=\mathbf 0$, a periodic linear system with all Floquet multipliers equal to one and a Jordan block, giving linear drift of $\mathbf R$ with a periodic wobble. No exponent has positive real part. Grade: derived for the spectrum; the statement that a small $\mathbf P$ does not destabilize the relative motion at second order is inferred, not proved, because the $O(\|\mathbf U\|^2)$ term feeds back into the radial equation with the period of $M(\mathbf r_0(T))$. Falsifier: a Cartesian integration of the full six-dimensional pair equations from the circle with a small common velocity whose relative separation departs from the circle secularly faster than the second-order estimate, recorded with its $\mathbf P$ and $\mathbf U$ histories in a subject file.

### 7.3 Reduced regularity at a full-Hessian singular separation

For opposite polarity the reduced problem passes $r=1$ and $r=\tfrac12$ without noticing (both reduced coefficients $1+1/r$, $1+1/(2r)$ are regular), yet the full acceleration solve there is not unique: on the mirror manifold the right side of $H\mathbf A=\mathbf b$ is antisymmetric under $\Phi$, hence orthogonal to the singular sum vector, so $\mathbf A$ exists but is determined only up to an arbitrary multiple of $(\mathbf e,\mathbf e)$ at $r=1$ (and of the transverse sum vectors at $r=\tfrac12$). The reduced problem masks this non-uniqueness. Under the frozen rule the history ends at $r=1$ with an obstruction event, and the reference values of 6.3 and 6.6 report the time and speed of arrival at $r=1$ precisely because that is where the full history stops. The reduced continuation through $r=1$ to contact (Section 5) is a statement about the reduced problem only and must not be reported as a history of the full law. Falsifier for the end-of-history claim: an explicit continuation rule for the common mode adopted by the operator, which would be a different case.

### 7.4 Preparations outside the declared domain at release, and bracket semantics

The $r_0=25$ circle has individual speed $0.1407>0.1$ and is outside the provisional speed domain at release although $\epsilon=0.04<0.05$; the $r_0=50$ circle sits $2.5\times10^{-4}$ below the speed bound. Every other benchmark is inside both bounds at release. Histories 6.3 and 6.6 leave the speed domain at $r=49.5$ and $r=50.52$ before the $\epsilon$ domain at $r=20$. The brackets in Section 6 are agreement brackets between two converged quadrature rules (and a closed form where one exists), not outward-rounded interval enclosures; a rigorous enclosure would need interval evaluation of the integrands, which this instrument does not do. Within the agreement brackets at least thirteen significant digits are common to all routes for every tabulated value.

### 7.5 Limits of this reference

It covers mirror pairs only; a non-mirror pair ($\mathbf P\neq\mathbf 0$) has a non-constant centre velocity and is not reduced here. It establishes no global persistence; the circles are exact solutions and the eccentric orbit is a periodic radial quadrature, which is a statement about the reduced planar problem for all time, but the full-law claim is only the linear spectrum of 7.2. It says nothing about Darwin's historical law, about delayed laws, or about physical binding.

Falsifiers for the derived formulas, each checkable in Node from the Section 10 functional alone: (i) a configuration with $r\notin\{1,\tfrac12\}$ at which the numerically assembled 6×6 Hessian is singular, or one at $r=1$ or $\tfrac12$ at which it is not; (ii) a mirror circle at the tabulated $r_0$ and $\omega$ whose Cartesian Euler–Lagrange residual, computed from the formula, exceeds rounding; (iii) a rest release whose numerically solved $H\mathbf A$ at $T=0$ differs from $\sigma\mathbf e/(r(r-\sigma))$; (iv) a radial history whose arrival time at $r=20$ from a subject instrument lies outside the brackets of 6.3, 6.4, 6.5 or 6.6 after that instrument has passed the zero-coupling control.
