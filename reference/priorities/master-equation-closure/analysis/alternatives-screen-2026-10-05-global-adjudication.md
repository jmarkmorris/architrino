$$

R=T-S=|q(T)+q(S)|,\qquad
n=\frac{q(T)+q(S)}R,\qquad D=1+n\cdot q'(S).
$$

The complete old past is exactly the circular tail with the compatible endpoint patch defined in the [finite secular source](../binary-research/analysis/alternatives-screen-2026-10-05-radial-rotating-fate.md#complete-compatible-preparation). This is an externally supplied compatible preparation, not a claim that the prescribed negative-time circle solves the equation.

Y''=-\frac{2^{3/2}N}{R_d^{3/2}D},\qquad
R_d=|Y(s)+Y(\sigma)|,\qquad s-\sigma=\epsilon R_d,
\qquad D=1+\epsilon N\cdot Y'(\sigma).
$$

The exact positive-torque geometry previously reconstructed uses complete subfield causal chords, not a near-circle approximation. It gives $h'>0$ and $h(s)\ge h(0)=1$ at every generated time while the solution remains strictly subfield. Define

Here $z$ is reciprocal square-root relative radius, not the shifted radial velocity used in the transition source. The exact reconstruction identities are

The [accepted transition](../binary-research/analysis/alternatives-screen-2026-10-05-radial-rotating-transition.md) supplies an actual first $J=j_*$ event for a fixed small $j_*>0$, with $a_b\asymp\epsilon^{-16/3}$ and $s_b\asymp\epsilon^{-9}$. Its corrected scalar differs from

## Uniform estimates on full source windows

Work provisionally in $0<z<4$, $|y|<4$ after release. Then $a\ge1$ and

$$
|Y'|=a^{-1/4}\sqrt{y^2+z^4}<17.
$$

The supplied old history has $|Y'|<2$ for the small family already admitted. Consequently the whole joined physical history has speed at most $17\epsilon$. Choose $17\epsilon<1/2$. The complete source gap is strictly monotone; its limits give one partner root, while strict speed chords exclude every ordinary self root. In particular

$$
\frac{2r}{1+17\epsilon}\le R_d\le\frac{2r}{1-17\epsilon},\qquad
D\ge1-17\epsilon,
\qquad |Y''|\le Cr^{-3/2}.
$$

These facts exclude older hidden roots and retain the complete preparation. The radial lower bound $r=az^{-2}>1/16$ supplies positive clearance throughout the provisional chart.

The first uniformity problem is that the delay grows with radius. Nevertheless, global speed gives $r(s)\le1+17s$ and

$$
\ell=s-\sigma\le4\epsilon r(s)\le4\epsilon(1+17s).
$$

For example, the auxiliary choice $\epsilon\le1/1024$ makes $\ell<s/2$ whenever $s\ge1$. Then $\sigma\ge s/2\ge1/2$, and the same bound at $\sigma$ makes its own source positive. This is only an explicit sufficient bound for removing the preparation from late sampling, not a numerical admission threshold for the whole theorem. The previously constructed transition satisfies $s_b>1$ when $\epsilon$ is sufficiently small. Its prior generated trajectory remains inside the provisional box, so every later source window and its source's window are covered by the same argument.

For every $q\in[\sigma,s]$, the global speed and delay bounds give

$$
|Y(q)-Y(s)|\le17\ell\le C\epsilon r(s).
$$

Thus all radii on a receiving window are comparable to the current radius, uniformly as $r/a\to\infty$. This alone does not compare the scales $a(q)$ and $a(s)$; the relative torque estimate is required.

Let $\Delta\theta$ be the actual lifted angular change over the causal window. Positive areal rate and the strict-speed half-plane lemma give $0<\Delta\theta<\pi/2$. Monotonicity of $h$ at generated times yields

$$
\Delta\theta=\int_\sigma^s\frac{h(q)}{r(q)^2}\,dq
\le\frac{Ch(s)\ell}{r(s)^2}.
$$

The exact torque is

$$
h'(s)=\frac{2^{3/2}r(s)r(\sigma)\sin\Delta\theta}{R_d^{5/2}D}.
$$

Using $\sin\Delta\theta\le\Delta\theta$ and $\ell=\epsilon R_d$ gives

$$
0<\frac{h'}h\le C\epsilon r^{-3/2}.
$$

The bound applies at every point of the current window because its own source is generated. Integrating over a window of length $O(\epsilon r)$ then gives

$$
\left|\log\frac{h(q)}{h(s)}\right|\le C\epsilon^2r^{-1/2}
=C\delta^2z.
$$

Since $z<4$, this implies a uniform relative estimate for $h$, and therefore for $a=h^{4/3}$. The provisional normalized bounds now sharpen the source speed to $|Y'(q)|\le Ca(s)^{-1/4}$. Integrating this sharper estimate gives $r(q)/r(s)=1+O(\delta)$ over the entire source interval. This closes the scale comparison without any upper bound on relative radius or a newly supplied source history.

## The transverse error retains its vanishing factor

The preceding complete-window bounds imply

$$
\Delta\theta=\frac{h(s)\ell}{r(s)^2}[1+O(\delta)].
$$

In the current radial/transverse axes, the source displacement gives

$$
N_t=-\frac{r(\sigma)}{R_d}\sin\Delta\theta
=-\epsilon v[1+O(\delta)].
$$

The sine replacement has a relative error of order $\Delta\theta^2$, which is bounded by $C\delta^2$ because $\Delta\theta\le C\epsilon v=C\delta z^2$. This verifies that the entire transverse error contains the current factor $v$, even when $z\to0$. Positivity of the radial chord component gives $N_r=1+O(\delta^2)$.

Integral Taylor bounds on the full interval use $|Y''|\le Cr^{-3/2}$ and $\ell=O(\epsilon r)$. The acceleration correction relative to the displacement, and its correction to the transmitter factor, have size at most $C\epsilon^2r^{-1/2}=C\delta^2z$. Therefore

$$
\frac{R_d}{2r}=1-\epsilon u+O(\delta^2),\qquad
D=1+\epsilon u+O(\delta^2),
$$

uniformly for $0<z<4$. Multiplying the norm, transmitter and direction factors gives

$$
A_r=-r^{-3/2}[1+\tfrac12\epsilon u+O(\delta^2)],
\qquad A_t=\epsilon v r^{-3/2}[1+O(\delta)].
$$

The coefficient $1/2$ is the difference between the exponent $3/2$ from the range factor and the coefficient one from the transmitter factor. It cannot be obtained by dropping the transmitter Jacobian. No derivative of an error term or of acceleration has been used.

Since $h'=rA_t$, the exact scale derivative obeys $a_\chi/a=(4/3)\delta+O(\delta^2)$. Differentiating the coordinate definitions, and retaining the exact central radial term $h^2/r^3$, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_\chi&=-\frac12y+\frac23\delta z+R_z,& |R_z|&\le C\delta^2z,\\
y_\chi&=z^3-1-\frac16\delta y+R_y,& |R_y|&\le C\delta^2,\\
\delta_\chi&=-\frac13\delta^2+R_\delta,& |R_\delta|&\le C\delta^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

For example, $z_\chi=(z/2)(a_\chi/a-r_\chi/r)$ and $r_\chi/r=y/z$, so its error has the factor $z$ exactly. In $y_\chi$, the direct radial correction $-\delta y/2$ combines with the changing velocity unit's $+\delta y/3$ to give $-\delta y/6$. The factor $r^{-3/2}=a^{-3/2}z^3$ cancels the weighted clock in the radial equation; the transverse factor $v$ makes the torque cancellation equally uniform. This is the necessary stronger estimate, not an isotropic error divided by a vanishing clock.

These remainders belong to the actual delayed trajectory and may depend on its history. All subsequent arguments require only the displayed bounds; no autonomous replacement or derivative bound on the remainders is assumed.

## Independent eccentric-cycle calculation

Set $\delta=0$ in the weighted comparison. Its vector field is

$$
F_0(z,y)=(-y/2,z^3-1),\qquad e=y^2/2+W(z),
$$

and $de/d\chi=0$ by direct differentiation. The mathematical extension to real $z$ has a quartic $W$ tending to positive infinity at both ends and a unique minimum $W(1)=-3/2$. Every level $e>-3/2$ is a smooth compact periodic oval. At $e=0$, its left turning point is $z=0$ with $W'(0)=-2$, so the weighted period is regular there. Negative comparison $z$ is only an auxiliary orbit coordinate.

For the positive-$z$ bound levels $-3/2<e<0$, define the orbit integrals

$$
I(e)=\oint y^2\,d\chi
=4\int_{z_-}^{z_+}\sqrt{2[e-W(z)]}\,dz,
\qquad
Q(e)=\oint d\chi
=4\int_{z_-}^{z_+}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{2[e-W(z)]}}.
$$

The factor four follows from $|z_\chi|=|y|/2$ and both branches of the oval. Differentiation of $I$ gives $I'=Q$; the zero endpoint integrands of $I$ remove endpoint contributions. The same formulas extend smoothly to any compact annulus above the scalar minimum, including levels above zero, using the real turning points of the quartic. Simple turning-point square-root substitutions justify differentiation and smooth dependence there.

The first-order scalar derivative is

$$
e_\chi=\delta F(z,y),\qquad
F=-y^2/6+(4/3)(z^4-z)
$$

for the mathematical first-order comparison. On a central orbit, $z^4-z=z\,y_\chi$. Periodic integration by parts gives

$$
\oint(z^4-z)\,d\chi=-\oint z_\chi y\,d\chi=\frac12I(e),
\qquad \oint F\,d\chi=\frac12I(e)>0.
$$

The leading changes per comparison cycle are therefore $\Delta e=\delta I/2$ and $\Delta\log a=(4/3)\delta Q$. These are leading coefficients, not exact finite increments. Their averaged ratio gives

$$
\frac{de}{d\log a}=\frac38\frac I Q,\qquad
\frac{d\log I}{d\log a}=\frac38.
$$

There is no noncircular central bound cycle with zero first-order gain. This fact alone does not create the initial oscillation; the actual finite transition supplies it.

At $e=0$, the integrand is $\sqrt{4z-z^4}$. With $t=z^3/4$, direct substitutions give

$$
I(0)=\frac{16}{3}\int_0^1t^{-1/2}(1-t)^{1/2}\,dt=\frac{8\pi}{3},
$$

$$
Q(0)=\frac23\,2^{1/3}\int_0^1t^{-5/6}(1-t)^{-1/2}\,dt
=\frac23\,2^{1/3}B(1/6,1/2).
$$

Both integrals are finite. Near the minimum the linear oscillator has frequency $\sqrt{3/2}$ in $\chi$, so $Q\to2\pi/\sqrt{3/2}$ and $I=[2\pi/\sqrt{3/2}](e+3/2)+O((e+3/2)^2)$. Thus the averaged amplitude exponent is $3/16$, agreeing with the local transition coefficient at comparison grade. The averaged escape-scale ratio $[I(0)/I(e_0)]^{8/3}$ is finite for each fixed noncircular initial comparison level.

The separate $p=2$ sign control can also be reconstructed directly: its first-order normalized system has $x_\eta=y-2\delta/x$, $y_\eta=x^{-3}-x^{-2}$. The central scalar derivative is $2\delta y_\eta/x$, whose periodic integral is $2\delta\oint y^2x^{-2}d\eta$. The stated central parametrization gives $I_2=\pi e_c^2$ and $Q_2=2\pi$. This checks the signs without importing an observer-level physical account or a delayed $p=2$ trajectory.

## A bounded orbit corrector for the actual solution

For the actual bounded-error system, scalar differentiation gives

$$
e_\chi=\delta F(z,y)+O(\delta^2).
$$

Choose $e_-=-3/2+\nu/2$. On the compact full central annulus $e\in[e_-,2]$, $I,Q$ are smooth and bounded, with $I$ bounded below by a positive number. The function $Q(e)F-I(e)/2$ has zero integral around every oval: its first term integrates to $Q(e)I(e)/2$, and its second to the same quantity. Hence there is a bounded smooth single-valued primitive $B$ along the central flow satisfying

$$
\nabla B\cdot F_0=Q(e)F-I(e)/2.
$$

This existence can be checked without an averaging theorem. Take the smooth section $z=1,y>0$, integrate the zero-mean function along one central revolution, and identify its equal endpoint values. Smooth dependence of the section, flow and finite period on $e$ gives a smooth function on the annulus; subtracting its orbit mean fixes an arbitrary additive function of $e$. The annulus stays away from the only equilibrium, so this construction and its needed derivatives are uniformly bounded. Its negative-$z$ part defines a comparison coordinate function, not a future physical path.

Define $\mathcal I=I(e)-\delta B$. Along the actual path,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal I_\chi
&=Q(e)[\delta F+O(\delta^2)]-\delta\nabla B\cdot[F_0+O(\delta)]-\delta_\chi B\\
&=\frac\delta2I(e)+O(\delta^2)>c\delta>0
\end{aligned}
$$

for sufficiently small $\epsilon$. This is a pathwise inequality for the actual equation. Bounded, history-dependent remainders suffice.

The entry interval is separated from $e_-$ by a fixed scalar gap and thus a fixed positive gap in $I$. Since $|\mathcal I-I(e)|\le C\delta\le C\epsilon$, monotonicity of $\mathcal I$ excludes a first lower exit. While $e\le1$, the scalar itself bounds $z<2$ and $|y|\le\sqrt5$, leaving a strict margin inside the provisional box. Also $-C\delta^2\le\delta_\chi\le-c\delta^2$, after possibly reducing the threshold.

If the weighted interval were infinite with $e\in[e_-,1]$ and $z>0$, those parameter inequalities imply $\delta\asymp(1+\chi)^{-1}$ up to constants depending on the fixed entry value, and in particular $\int\delta\,d\chi=\infty$. The strictly increasing $\mathcal I$ would then exceed its bounded annular range. Thus either $e=1$ occurs at finite weighted time or the positive-radius coordinate tends to zero at finite weighted time.

There is no missing finite weighted endpoint with positive limiting $z$. The bounded vector field gives limits of $z,y,\delta$ there. The inequality $\delta_\chi\ge-C\delta^2$ bounds $\delta$ away from zero on finite weighted intervals, so $a=(\epsilon/\delta)^4$ is finite. If $z$ also has a positive limit, $ds/d\chi$ is finite, all ordinary source margins remain strict, and local continuation extends the path. This closes that endpoint alternative.

## The positive scalar passage and exhaustive terminal alternatives

Suppose the actual scalar first reaches $e=1$ with $z>0$. Every central initial point on that compact positive-$z$ arc crosses $z=0$ with outgoing $y=\sqrt2$ within a uniformly bounded weighted time. A fixed negative-$z$ sample on the same central oval is reached within one period, regardless of the starting phase. The full central level has strict margins inside $|z|<3$, $|y|<4$.

On that fixed finite interval, the actual system differs from $F_0$ by $O(\epsilon)$ while it remains positive in $z$. Subtraction of the integral equations and the Lipschitz bound of the polynomial $F_0$ give an $O(\epsilon)$ path difference by Grönwall's inequality. The actual history dependence is harmless here: its bounded perturbation is estimated along the path and is not replaced by an autonomous function. For sufficiently small $\epsilon$, the strict box margins prevent an outer exit. Remaining positive until the central negative sample would contradict the path bound. Therefore the actual path has a finite limiting weighted time at $z=0$ first. Near a zero of $z$ on the central level, $|y|$ is bounded away from zero; approach from positive actual $z$ requires the outgoing sign. Hence this passage has a positive limiting $y$.

Combining the annular argument and this fixed-time comparison gives a finite $\chi_\infty$ with

$$
z\to0,\qquad y\to y_\infty\ge0,\qquad
\delta\to\delta_\infty>0,\qquad a\to a_\infty<\infty.
$$

Bounded derivatives give the limits; $\delta_\chi\ge-C\delta^2$ gives the positive parameter limit. A negative $y_\infty$ would give $z_\chi>c>0$ close to the endpoint, contradicting a positive coordinate approaching zero. A prior arrival at $z=0$ before the $e=1$ passage can have $y_\infty=0$; it must not be discarded by assuming transversality.

For the positive-velocity case, $R_z=O(\delta^2z)$ gives $z_\chi\to-y_\infty/2<0$. Writing $\Delta=\chi_\infty-\chi$, it follows that $z\asymp\Delta$. Since $a\to a_\infty>0$,

$$
\frac{ds}{d\chi}=a^{5/4}z^{-3}\asymp\Delta^{-3},\qquad
s\asymp\Delta^{-2},\qquad r=az^{-2}\asymp s.
$$

For the zero-velocity case, the actual second equation implies $-2<y_\chi<-1/2$ sufficiently near the endpoint: $z,y\to0$ and the uniform $C\delta^2$ error is small. No limiting error value is needed. Thus $y\asymp\Delta>0$. The first equation has the exact bounded-coefficient form

$$
z_\chi=-y/2+b(\chi)z,\qquad |b|\le C\epsilon,
$$

because its remainder is proportional to $z$. Its backward integrating-factor formula from limiting value $z(\chi_\infty)=0$ expresses $z(\chi)$ as a positive integral of $y/2$ times a uniformly bounded positive exponential. Therefore $z\asymp\Delta^2$, and

$$
\frac{ds}{d\chi}\asymp\Delta^{-6},\qquad
s\asymp\Delta^{-5},\qquad r\asymp\Delta^{-4}\asymp s^{4/5}.
$$

Physical time is a fixed positive multiple of $s$ for each fixed launch, so both cases cover its whole infinite future. They are not physical finite-time endpoints. Conversely, ordinary evolution cannot fail at a finite physical time inside these bounds: speed bounds position, $r>1/16$ keeps a positive delay, the complete past bounds source times below, and source denominators stay above $1-17\epsilon$. All sampled position and velocity histories are regular on the resulting compact source windows. The original equation's method of steps supplies uniqueness and continuation.

Finally, $\theta_\chi=z$ and the weighted interval is finite, so total angular advance is finite. The exact velocity formula gives

$$
q'(T)=\epsilon a^{-1/4}[y\,n(\theta)+z^2t(\theta)]
\longrightarrow\epsilon a_\infty^{-1/4}y_\infty n(\theta_\infty).
$$

The partner limit is its negative. This proves the asserted terminal velocity, with no claim that its magnitude must be strictly positive.

## Defects, falsifiers and remaining boundaries

The only correction identified is the time scope of $a\ge1$: it covers generated times, not the supplied negative-time patch. That restriction is demonstrated by the exact patch derivative and is already sufficient for every global estimate. The complete old past retains its separate speed bound; the explicit late-window argument excludes it from the estimates that use monotone generated $h$. No subject file was changed by this assessment.

The following observations would falsify the respective derived steps:

- A complete admitted source window, or its generated source's window, violating the relative torque estimate or resulting scale comparison as $z\to0$.
- A transverse response error that remains nonzero relative to $\epsilon v r^{-3/2}$ when $v\to0$, or a weighted-coordinate remainder larger than its displayed uniform bound.
- Failure of the exact orbit identity $\oint F=I/2$, of $I'=Q$, or of the bounded single-valued corrector on an annulus separated from the central minimum.
- A path satisfying the uniform remainder bounds that crosses the lower annular boundary or remains there for infinite weighted time despite the positive corrected-integral derivative.
- A finite physical-time endpoint with the established speed, separation, root and source-window margins intact.
- A zero-terminal-velocity endpoint lacking the proportional-$z$ remainder or the strict negative bound on $y_\chi$; either would invalidate the quadratic $z$ scaling used here.

An admitted counterexample for the exact compatible sufficiently small family would overturn the global theorem. A finite-speed numerical discrepancy without a certified admission threshold and a controlled trajectory error is not such a counterexample. The comparison alone does not establish global fate; the actual-window bounds, corrector and physical-time reconstruction are necessary parts of the accepted proof.

Whether the terminal magnitude is positive or zero for a specified launch remains unresolved. Quantitative launch thresholds, terminal coefficients, nonmirror robustness and other response laws are outside this assessment. The all-future dispersal theorem does settle the previous fixed-launch fate gap for this particular sufficiently small family.
