## 1. Law, solve and the two residuals

Six members $i=0,\dots,5$ carry polarities $q_i\in\{+1,-1\}$, three of each sign, with positions $\mathbf X_i$ and velocities $\mathbf V_i$ in the void frame and $T$ absolute time. For a pair, $\sigma_{ij}=q_iq_j$, $d_{ij}=\|\mathbf X_i-\mathbf X_j\|$, $\mathbf e_{ij}=(\mathbf X_i-\mathbf X_j)/d_{ij}$, $\mathbf w_{ij}=\mathbf V_i-\mathbf V_j$, $\dot d_{ij}=\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot\mathbf w_{ij}$ and $\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}=\mathbf w_{ij}-\dot d_{ij}\mathbf e_{ij}$. The selected law, with $\lambda_{\mathrm W}=-1/2$, $\mu_{\mathrm W}=1$, coupling $K$ and wake speed $c_f$ (both $1$ in every number below), unit integration weights, distinct present-time partners, no self term, no softening and no ceiling, is

$$
\mathbf A_i=\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\sigma_{ij}K}{d_{ij}^2}\left[1-\frac{\dot d_{ij}^2}{2c_f^2}+\frac{d_{ij}\ddot d_{ij}}{c_f^2}\right]\mathbf e_{ij},\qquad \ddot d_{ij}=\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf A_i-\mathbf A_j)+\frac{\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2}{d_{ij}} .
$$

Because $\ddot d_{ij}$ contains the unknown accelerations, every evaluation is a linear solve. Substituting the identity and separating the known part of each bracket from the part carrying accelerations gives, for the stacked unknown $\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^{18}$,

$$
M\mathbf a=\mathbf b,\qquad M=I_{18}-\sum_{i<j}\alpha_{ij}\mathbf u_{ij}\mathbf u_{ij}^{\mathsf T},\qquad \alpha_{ij}=\frac{\sigma_{ij}K\mu_{\mathrm W}}{c_f^2d_{ij}},\qquad \mathbf b=\sum_{i<j}\beta_{ij}\mathbf u_{ij},\qquad \beta_{ij}=\frac{\sigma_{ij}K}{d_{ij}^2}\left[1+\lambda_{\mathrm W}\frac{\dot d_{ij}^2}{c_f^2}+\mu_{\mathrm W}\frac{\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2}{c_f^2}\right],
\tag{1.1}
$$

where $\mathbf u_{ij}$ has $\mathbf e_{ij}$ in block $i$, $-\mathbf e_{ij}$ in block $j$ and zeros elsewhere. The matrix depends on positions only; velocities enter only $\mathbf b$. The lane's instrument, [weber-binding-sphere-reference-lib.mjs](../evidence/weber-binding-sphere-reference-lib.mjs) with [weber-binding-sphere-reference-sixbody.mjs](../evidence/weber-binding-sphere-reference-sixbody.mjs), assembles (1.1) by a loop over ordered pairs in the frozen [law module](../../binary-research/evidence/weber-frequency-reference-law.mjs) of the binary reference, solves it by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting (determinant from the pivots), and checks every solved acceleration against the law without the matrix, by inserting it into the right-hand side with the full $\ddot d$ identity. No code is taken from any other instrument in the repository; the only external numerical routine is `numpy.linalg.eigvals` in the shared venv, used for the spectra of Section 4 after the Jacobians were assembled here.

Two residuals are used. For a rigid rotation at angular rate $\Omega$ about a unit axis $\mathbf n$ through the origin, the candidate acceleration is the centripetal $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}$ with $\mathbf X_{i,\perp}=\mathbf X_i-(\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n$, the velocities are $\Omega\mathbf n\times\mathbf X_i$, and the rigid residual is $\max_i\|\mathbf A_i+\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}\|/(\Omega^2R_{\max})$ with $\mathbf A_i$ from the full solve. For a prescribed spherical path of radius $R$ with every member at speed $\Omega R$ on a great circle, the prescribed-path residual is

$$
\mathcal R=\max_{T}\max_i\frac{\|\mathbf A_i^{\mathrm{law}}(\mathbf X(T),\mathbf V(T))+\Omega^2\mathbf X_i(T)\|}{\Omega^2R},
\tag{1.2}
$$

sampled at $64$ equally spaced times over one period $2\pi/\Omega$, with the residual vector decomposed along $\hat{\mathbf X}_i$ (radial; since $\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ is radial this component equals $(\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i+v^2)/(\Omega^2R^2)$ with $v=\Omega R$), along $\hat{\mathbf V}_i$ (tangential, $\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i/(v\Omega^2R)$) and along $\hat{\mathbf X}_i\times\hat{\mathbf V}_i$ (the remaining tangent component), each reported as its own maximum.

## 3. The rigid reduction, stated and proved

**Statement.** Let a configuration rotate rigidly at rate $\Omega$ about a unit axis $\mathbf n$ through the origin, so $\mathbf V_i=\Omega\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf X_i$, and suppose the acceleration matrix $M$ of (1.1) is invertible on it. Then the solved accelerations equal the centripetal accelerations $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}$ if and only if the instantaneous inverse-square balance $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}=\sum_{j\ne i}\sigma_{ij}K\mathbf e_{ij}/d_{ij}^2$ holds for every $i$. On such a rotation every $\dot d_{ij}$ vanishes identically and, when the balance holds, every $\ddot d_{ij}$ vanishes too, so the velocity terms of the law are invisible to the balance for any $\lambda_{\mathrm W},\mu_{\mathrm W}$. Moreover, equal speed on a rigid rotation forces equal axial distance.

*Proof.* Write $\boldsymbol\rho_{ij}=\mathbf X_i-\mathbf X_j$ and $\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}$ for its component perpendicular to $\mathbf n$. The relative velocity is $\mathbf w_{ij}=\Omega\,\mathbf n\times\boldsymbol\rho_{ij}$, which is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol\rho_{ij}$, so $\dot d_{ij}=\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot\mathbf w_{ij}=0$ and $\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2=\|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2=\Omega^2\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2$. Insert the candidate $\mathbf a^\ast_i=-\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}$ into the left side of (1.1). Since $\mathbf u_{ij}\cdot\mathbf a^\ast=-\Omega^2\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf X_{i,\perp}-\mathbf X_{j,\perp})=-\Omega^2\boldsymbol\rho_{ij}\cdot\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}/d_{ij}=-\Omega^2\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2/d_{ij}$, the kinematic identity gives $\ddot d_{ij}=\mathbf u_{ij}\cdot\mathbf a^\ast+\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2/d_{ij}=0$ for the candidate, and

$$
M\mathbf a^\ast=\mathbf a^\ast+\sum_{i<j}\alpha_{ij}\Omega^2\frac{\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2}{d_{ij}}\mathbf u_{ij}=\mathbf a^\ast+\sum_{i<j}\frac{\sigma_{ij}K\mu_{\mathrm W}\Omega^2\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2}{c_f^2d_{ij}^2}\mathbf u_{ij},
$$

while the right side, with $\dot d_{ij}=0$, is $\mathbf b=\sum_{i<j}\big(\sigma_{ij}K/d_{ij}^2\big)\mathbf u_{ij}+\sum_{i<j}\big(\sigma_{ij}K\mu_{\mathrm W}\Omega^2\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2/(c_f^2d_{ij}^2)\big)\mathbf u_{ij}$. The $\mu_{\mathrm W}$ sums are identical and cancel, so $M\mathbf a^\ast=\mathbf b$ exactly when $\mathbf a^\ast=\sum_{i<j}(\sigma_{ij}K/d_{ij}^2)\mathbf u_{ij}$, which block by block is the inverse-square balance. Since $M$ is invertible the solve has one solution, so the candidate is that solution exactly when the balance holds, and then every $\ddot d_{ij}=0$ by the computation above. Finally, the speed of member $i$ is $\Omega\|\mathbf X_{i,\perp}\|$, so equal speeds mean equal $\|\mathbf X_{i,\perp}\|$; with $\|\mathbf X_i\|=R$ fixed this is equal $|\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf n|$, so the members lie on one circle $z=0$ or on two circles $z=\pm z_0$ of the same radius $a$, $R^2=a^2+z_0^2$. $\square$

The ring analysis proved the same cancellation for a planar ring about an in-plane-perpendicular axis; the argument above needs only $\mathbf u_{ij}\cdot\mathbf X_\perp=\|\boldsymbol\rho_{ij,\perp}\|^2/d_{ij}$ and holds for any axis through the origin and any configuration, planar or not. Its numerical counterpart in Section 4 is that the solved hexagon accelerations have every $|\ddot d_{ij}|\le2.1\times10^{-14}$ at the balanced rate and a relative residual of order $10^{-1}$ at $1.1\,\Omega$.

> Claim grade: derived. Falsifier: a rigidly rotating state with invertible $M$ on which the full solve returns the centripetal accelerations while the inverse-square balance fails by more than rounding, or the converse.

## 4. F0: the planar alternating hexagon

Six members at angles $k\pi/3$ on a circle of radius $\rho$ with polarity $(-1)^k$, rotating rigidly at $\Omega$ about the axis of the circle. By the rigid reduction the balance is the inverse-square one. Member $0$ at $\rho\hat{\mathbf x}$ sees its two neighbours ($k=1,5$, opposite polarity, separation $2\rho\sin(\pi/6)=\rho$), its two second neighbours ($k=2,4$, like polarity, separation $2\rho\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt3\rho$) and its antipode ($k=3$, opposite polarity, separation $2\rho$). The outward radial component of $\mathbf e_{0k}$ is $\sin(k\pi/6)$ for $k=1,2,3$, that is $1/2$, $\sqrt3/2$ and $1$. The outward radial acceleration is therefore

$$
\frac{K}{\rho^2}\left[-2\cdot\frac12+\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac14\right]=-\frac{K}{\rho^2}\left(\frac54-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right),
$$

inward, and the tangential components cancel in pairs because the reflection through member $0$'s diameter exchanges $k$ with $6-k$, which carry the same polarity. Equating to $-\Omega^2\rho$,

$$
\boxed{\ \Omega^2(\rho)=\left(\frac54-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)\frac{K}{\rho^3}\approx0.672649730810374\,\frac{K}{\rho^3},\qquad v=\Omega\rho=c_f\sqrt{\frac{5/4-1/\sqrt3}{x}},\qquad x=\frac{\rho c_f^2}{K},\ }
\tag{4.1}
$$

so the hexagon balances at every radius, and the member speed equals $c_f$ at the equality radius $x_\ast=5/4-1/\sqrt3\approx0.672650$; the ring is strict-subfield for $x>x_\ast$. This closed form was derived here before the preregistration's withheld coefficient was compared, and the instrument confirms it: at $\rho\in\{0.3,0.5,1,2,3\}$ the full $18\times18$ solve on the rotating hexagon returns $\mathbf A_i=-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ with normalized residual $\le2.4\times10^{-15}$, tangential component on member $0$ at most $6.4\times10^{-15}$, every $|\ddot d_{ij}|\le2.1\times10^{-14}$, and a relative residual between $9.2\times10^{-2}$ and $1.6\times10^{-1}$ at $1.1\,\Omega$.

The acceleration matrix reduces by the sixfold symmetry, which maps $k\to k+1$ and preserves every $\sigma_{ij}$ because both polarities advance together. In each member's local frame $(\hat{\mathbf r}_k,\hat{\mathbf t}_k,\hat{\mathbf z})$ the discrete Fourier modes of character $k\in\{0,\dots,5\}$ decouple into a $2\times2$ in-plane block and a $1\times1$ axial block; the axial blocks are the identity because no $\mathbf e_{ij}$ has an axial component. With pair types $m=1,2,3$ (half-angles $m\pi/6$, separations $2x\sin(m\pi/6)$, signs $-,+,-$), the quadratic form of $M$ on a mode evaluates, per member, to $|A|^2+|B|^2-\sum_m\alpha_mw_m\,|s_m(1+\omega^{mk})A+c_m(\omega^{mk}-1)B|^2$ with $\omega=e^{i\pi/3}$, $s_m=\sin(m\pi/6)$, $c_m=\cos(m\pi/6)$, $w_m=1$ for $m=1,2$ and $w_3=1/2$. Evaluating the blocks (sympy in the venv, [weber-binding-sphere-reference-hexagon-blocks.py](../evidence/weber-binding-sphere-reference-hexagon-blocks.py), a copy of the scratch script run at 00:02Z) gives the closed forms

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k=0$ | $\operatorname{diag}\!\big(1+\tfrac{2-\sqrt3}{x},\,1\big)$ | breathing $1+(2-\sqrt3)/x$; rotation $1$ | $1+(2-\sqrt3)/x$ |
| $k=1,5$ | $\begin{pmatrix}1+\tfrac{3-\sqrt3}{4x}&\mp i\tfrac{3-\sqrt3}{4x}\\ \pm i\tfrac{3-\sqrt3}{4x}&1+\tfrac{3-\sqrt3}{4x}\end{pmatrix}$ | translation $1$; sublattice shift $1+(3-\sqrt3)/(2x)$ | $1+(3-\sqrt3)/(2x)$ each |

Multiplying the blocks (the six axial eigenvalues are $1$),

$$
\det M=\left(1+\frac{2-\sqrt3}{x}\right)\left(1-\frac{\sqrt3}{x}\right)\left(1+\frac3x\right)\left(1+\frac{3-\sqrt3}{2x}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{7-\sqrt3}{2x}+\frac{9-5\sqrt3}{4x^2}\right)^{2}.

$$

Every factor but one is positive for $x>0$ ($9-5\sqrt3>0$), so the matrix is singular exactly at $x=\sqrt3$, where the kernel is the $k=3$ radial mode $a_k\propto(-1)^k$: the positive sublattice expands while the negative one contracts. For $x<\sqrt3$ that single eigenvalue is negative and $M$ is indefinite but invertible. The singular radius $x=\sqrt3$ lies in the strict-subfield part of the family ($v=0.623\,c_f$ there) and, as for the square at $x=1$, the balanced hexagon at that radius is a solution of the law but not a well-posed history, because the right side is orthogonal to the kernel and the acceleration along the kernel is undetermined.

## 5. F1: rigid two-circle arrangements

Three members on $z=+z_0$ and three on $z=-z_0$, all at cylindrical radius $a$ (so $R^2=a^2+z_0^2$), free angles, rigid rotation about $\hat{\mathbf z}$. By Section 3 a rigid rotation is exact if and only if the inverse-square balance holds, and its axial component must vanish because the centripetal acceleration has none.

**Segregated class ($+++$ over $---$): one-signed axial obstruction.** Let member $i$ lie on the upper circle. A same-circle partner $j$ has $\sigma_{ij}=+1$ and $z_j=z_i$, so $\mathbf e_{ij}$ has no axial component and contributes nothing axially. A lower-circle partner has $\sigma_{ij}=-1$ and axial separation $z_i-z_j=2z_0>0$, so its contribution is $-K\mathbf e_{ij}/d_{ij}^2$ with axial component $-2Kz_0/d_{ij}^3<0$. The axial inverse-square sum on every upper member is therefore $-2Kz_0\sum_{j\ \mathrm{lower}}d_{ij}^{-3}<0$ for every $z_0>0$ and every choice of angles, and symmetrically positive on every lower member. Since balance requires it to vanish, no rigid two-circle relative equilibrium exists in the segregated class with $z_0>0$. The limit $z_0\to0$ is a single circle carrying $+++---$ in some order, which is a one-circle family and not a two-circle one. The instrument evaluated the axial inverse-square sums on five seeded random states ($z_0\in[0.0117,0.504]$): every upper value negative and every lower value positive, for example `z0=0.5041: axial inverse-square sums = -1.866 -1.218 -0.5615 0.4251 1.402 1.818`. The full solve on these unbalanced states has $\ddot d_{ij}\ne0$ and its axial components are not sign-constrained; that is why the check is of the inverse-square sum, which is the quantity the reduction theorem makes decisive.

> Claim grade: derived. Falsifier: a segregated two-circle state with $z_0>0$ whose axial inverse-square sum vanishes on some upper member.

**Mixed class ($++-$ over $--+$).** Here an upper member of either sign has both attracting and repelling lower partners and the axial sum is not one-signed, so the question is numerical. The lane's own Levenberg–Marquardt (central-difference Jacobian, bounded parameters) minimized the sum of squared components of $\mathbf A_i+\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}$ (full solve, normalized by $\Omega^2$) over the five free angles ($\theta_0=0$), $\ln z_0$ with $z_0/a\in[0.01,10]$ and $\ln\Omega$ with $\Omega\in[0.05,20]$, at $a=1$, from $120$ seeded random starts (seed $20261005$, own mulberry32 generator). The best normalized residual is

$$
\min\ \max_i\frac{\|\mathbf A_i+\Omega^2\mathbf X_{i,\perp}\|}{\Omega^2R}=2.7721\times10^{-2}\quad\text{at }z_0/a=0.01\ \text{(box edge)},\ \Omega=0.820089,\ \text{angles }(0,\,4.1890,\,5.2361\mid3.1416,\,7.3306\equiv1.0474,\,2.0945),
$$

reached by eight of the $120$ starts (the next value is $5.37\times10^{-2}$; the median over starts is $0.25$). ## 6. F1′: the octahedron about a body diagonal, prism and antiprism

The regular octahedron with vertices $\pm R\hat{\mathbf x},\pm R\hat{\mathbf y},\pm R\hat{\mathbf z}$, $R=1$, rotating about the threefold axis $(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$, has every vertex at axial distance $R\sqrt{2/3}$: it is the triangular antiprism with $z_0/a=1/\sqrt2$, and the two triangles are $\{+\hat{\mathbf x},+\hat{\mathbf y},+\hat{\mathbf z}\}$ and $\{-\hat{\mathbf x},-\hat{\mathbf y},-\hat{\mathbf z}\}$. "Antipodal opposite polarity" leaves one binary choice per axis; modulo overall reversal the inequivalent assignments about this axis are the segregated one ($+\hat{\mathbf x},+\hat{\mathbf y},+\hat{\mathbf z}$ all positive, the upper triangle $+++$) and the mixed one (one of the three reversed, upper triangle $++-$), and both were evaluated, since the task names both the antipodal-polarity and the polarity-segregated octahedron and about this axis the former contains the latter.

Both assignments fail. The segregated octahedron is excluded by Section 5: its axial inverse-square sums are $-0.9608$ on the three upper vertices and $+0.9608$ on the lower ones. The mixed octahedron has axial sums $(-0.1443,-0.1443,+0.6722)$ above and the negatives below, nonzero on every vertex, so it is not balanced either. The full-solve rigid residual as a function of $\Omega$ is the same curve for both assignments to all printed digits (every vertex has two like and two opposite neighbours at $\sqrt2R$ and an opposite antipode at $2R$ in both, with congruent local environments differently oriented to the axis): $462$ at $\Omega=0.05$, $6.514$ at $0.473$, $5.971$ at the isolated-pair value $0.5$, $2.527$ at $1$, $1.450$ at $4.47$, $1.3966$ at the box edge $\Omega=20$, decreasing toward the limit $1.394$ as $\Omega\to\infty$, where the $\mu_{\mathrm W}\|\mathbf w_\perp\|^2$ term dominates both sides. There is no minimum inside the box and the residual never approaches the candidate threshold.

The triangular prism (upper and lower triangles aligned) and antiprism (offset by $\pi/3$), both polarity classes, were scanned on $z_0\in\{1/60,\dots,59/60\}$ with $a=\sqrt{1-z_0^2}$ and $\Omega\in[0.05,20]$ (201 logarithmic points). The best residuals, $2.65\times10^{-2}$ (segregated, both solids), $2.90\times10^{-2}$ (mixed prism) and $2.93\times10^{-2}$ (mixed antiprism), all sit at the scan edge $z_0=0.9833$, $\Omega=20$, where the two tiny triangles near the poles rotate fast and the normalized residual again approaches its large-$\Omega$ limit; none is a candidate.

> Claim grade: measured on the stated boxes; the segregated exclusions are derived through Section 5. Falsifier: an $18\times18$ solve on one of these solids at some $\Omega$ with rigid residual below $10^{-8}$.

## 8. F3: the jet landscape on the unit sphere

For a path to stay on the sphere $\|\mathbf X_i\|=R$ at constant speed $v$, the solved acceleration must satisfy $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i+v^2=0$ (second derivative of $\|\mathbf X_i\|^2/2$) and $\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=0$ (derivative of $v^2/2$) at every time. At $T=0$ these are two scalar conditions per member that any shooting start must meet before it can be a candidate, and the "jet residual" $J_0=\max_i\max\big(|\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i+v^2|/v^2,\ |\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i|R/v^3\big)$ measures how far a prepared state is from meeting them. For each $v\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0\}$ the instrument prepared $1000$ seeded states (seed $20261005$) in the unconstrained polarity stratum ($q=(+,+,+,-,-,-)$) with $\|\mathbf X_i\|=1$, $\sum\mathbf X_i=0$, tangential velocities of speed $v$ and $\sum\mathbf V_i=0$ (alternating projections to $10^{-12}$; no redraw was needed), solved the $18\times18$ system and evaluated $J_0$. This is a landscape, not a search.

### 14.1 The linearization and its sector blocks

The balanced hexagon is an equilibrium of the rotating-frame Lagrangian $L_{\mathrm{rot}}(\mathbf Y,\dot{\mathbf Y})=L(\mathbf Y,\dot{\mathbf Y}+\Omega J\mathbf Y)$, where $L=\tfrac12\sum\|\mathbf V_i\|^2-U-\sum_{i<j}(\sigma_{ij}K/d_{ij})\,\dot d_{ij}^2/(2c_f^2)$ with $U=\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}K/d_{ij}$ is the Lagrangian whose Euler–Lagrange equations are the frozen law ($\lambda_{\mathrm W}=-\mu_{\mathrm W}/2$). Exactly as in Section 5.1 of the [ring analysis](weber-overnight-ring.md#51-the-rotating-frame-second-variation), on the slice $\dot{\mathbf Y}=\mathbf 0$ every $\dot d_{ij}=\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot\Omega J(\mathbf Y_i-\mathbf Y_j)$ vanishes identically for a planar configuration rotating about its normal, so the Weber term contributes nothing to the mixed or position Hessians there, and the second variation is the gyroscopic system

$$
M\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}+2\Omega J\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}+\mathcal K\boldsymbol\xi=0,\qquad \mathcal K=\nabla^2U(\mathbf Y^\ast)-\Omega^2\Pi,
\tag{14.1}
$$

with $M$ the acceleration matrix of Section 4 (the velocity Hessian of $L$), $\Pi$ the in-plane projector and $J$ the quarter turn acting on each member's in-plane displacement. The sixfold operation (rotation by $60^\circ$ with the member shift $k\to k+1$, which flips every polarity and is a symmetry because the law is invariant under a global flip) commutes with (14.1), so the problem decomposes on the local-frame Fourier modes $(a_j,b_j,c_j)=(A,B,C)\,\omega^{jk}$, $\omega=e^{i\pi/3}$, into a $2\times2$ in-plane block and a $1\times1$ axial block per character $k$; the reflection $z\to-z$ decouples the axial component, on which $M$ is the identity and $J$ and the centrifugal term vanish. Writing the Hessian of $\sigma K/d$ on a pair as $(\sigma K/d^3)(3\mathbf e\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}-I)$ and evaluating all three forms on a mode by pair type $m\in\{1,2,3\}$ (angle $m\pi/3$, separation $d_m=2x\sin(m\pi/6)$, sign $(-1)^m$, weight $1$ for $m=1,2$ and $\tfrac12$ for $m=3$, per-member normalization), with $x=\rho c_f^2/K$ and $\Omega^2=(5/4-1/\sqrt3)K/\rho^3$, the blocks are (in-plane $\mathcal K$ in units $K/\rho^3$, basis $(A,B)$ = radial, tangential; the sign choice $\pm$ is for $k=1$ and $k=5$, respectively $k=2$ and $k=4$):

### 14.2 The $k=3$ sector in closed form and the theorem

The $k=3$ blocks are diagonal, so with $s=z^2$ the in-plane characteristic polynomial is $c_2s^2+c_1s+c_0=0$ with

$$
c_2=m_Am_B=\frac{(x+3)(x-\sqrt3)}{x^2},\qquad
c_1=m_Ak_B+m_Bk_A+4\Omega^2=\frac{(123\sqrt3+63)-(6+4\sqrt3)\,x}{12\,x^4},\qquad
c_0=k_Ak_B=-\frac{29\,(7+4\sqrt3)}{16\,x^6},
\tag{14.2}
$$

and discriminant

$$
x^8\left(c_1^2-4c_2c_0\right)=\frac{(704\sqrt3+1232)x^2+(540\sqrt3+948)x+(1962-1071\sqrt3)}{24}>0\quad\text{for every }x>0,
\tag{14.3}
$$

since $1962-1071\sqrt3=106.97>0$ and the other coefficients are positive. Hence both roots $s_\pm$ are real at every $x>0$.

**Theorem.** The balanced hexagon has a rotating-frame eigenvalue with positive real part in the $k=3$ sector at every $x\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{\sqrt3\}$.

*Proof.* $c_0<0$ for every $x$. For $x>\sqrt3$, $c_2>0$, so $s_+s_-=c_0/c_2<0$: exactly one root $s_+>0$, and $z=\sqrt{s_+}$ is a positive real eigenvalue. For $0<x<\sqrt3$, $c_2<0$, so $s_+s_-=c_0/c_2>0$ and both real roots have the same sign; their sum is $-c_1/c_2$, and $c_1>0$ on $0<x<(123\sqrt3+63)/(6+4\sqrt3)=21.35$, so for $x<\sqrt3$ the sum is positive: both roots are positive and the sector has two positive real eigenvalues $\sqrt{s_+}>\sqrt{s_-}>0$. $\square$

Consequences read off the same polynomial. As $x\to\sqrt3^-$ the larger root diverges, $s_+\sim-c_1/c_2\to+\infty$ (the fast rate of the indefinite side: $81\,\Omega$ at $x=0.999\sqrt3$), while $s_-\to-c_0/c_1$ stays finite ($0.399$ at $x\to\sqrt3$, that is $1.75\,\Omega$ just above $\sqrt3$); as $x\to\sqrt3^+$ the divergent root becomes a fast oscillation ($s_-\to-\infty$). In the inverse-square control limit $x\to\infty$, $c_1/\Omega^2\to-(46+28\sqrt3)/59=-1.60165$ and $c_0/\Omega^4\to-55.795$, so $s_+/\Omega^2\to8.3132$ and the growth rate tends to $2.8833\,\Omega$; the Weber matrix $M$ only rescales the rate, as for the ring. Values at the reference radii: $1.1653\,\Omega$ ($x=0.3$), $2.0421\,\Omega$ (equality radius), $3.0032\,\Omega$ ($x=1$), $2.0455\,\Omega$ ($x=3$), $2.5315\,\Omega$ ($x=10$), which are exactly the largest real parts of the frozen full spectra of Section 4 and of the subject: the fastest growth of the hexagon is in the $k=3$ sector at every radius examined.

The $k=0$ sector is elliptic at every radius: eliminating the rotation coordinate as in the ring gives $m_a\ddot a+\Omega^2a=0$, so the breathing frequency is $\omega_{\mathrm{br}}^2=\Omega^2x/(x+2-\sqrt3)$ ($0.888\,\Omega$ at $x=1$, $0.958\,\Omega$ at $x=3$, the values in the frozen spectra), with the remaining roots $0$ (rotation phase and family radius; axial translation and boost). The $k=1\oplus5$ and $k=2\oplus4$ sectors have complex Hermitian blocks; their quartic characteristic polynomials in $z$ are in the receipt (`charpoly_in_z`) and were not sign-analysed in closed form. Numerically they carry positive real parts at all ten radii $x\in\{0.1,0.3,x_\ast,1,1.5,1.7,1.74,2,3,10\}$ (largest real parts between $0.60\,\Omega$ and $1.32\,\Omega$ for $k=1\oplus5$, between $0.61\,\Omega$ and $1.91\,\Omega$ for $k=2\oplus4$), always below the $k=3$ rate.

## 15. Necessary conditions and an obstruction attempt for non-rigid equal-speed spherical histories (blind, Part 2c(b))

Status: derivations only, written blind to the geometry lane's round-2 material; nothing here was computed numerically except where stated. The question: six members of polarities $(+,+,+,-,-,-)$ moving under the frozen law for all time with $\|\mathbf X_i\|=R$ and $\|\mathbf V_i\|=v$ constant, $\sum\mathbf X_i=\sum\mathbf V_i=\mathbf 0$, not a rigid rotation. Throughout, $K=c_f=1$ in the formulas, and the two kinematic identities of such a history are $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf V_i=0$, $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=-v^2$ and $\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=0$ for every member and time.

### 15.1 What is already known, re-derived

The pair contribution is $c_{ij}\mathbf e_{ij}$ with the symmetric scalar $c_{ij}=(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij}^2)[1-\dot d_{ij}^2/2+d_{ij}\ddot d_{ij}]$, so $\mathbf A_i=\sum_{j\ne i}c_{ij}\mathbf e_{ij}$. With $G=\tfrac12\sum\|\mathbf X_i\|^2-\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}d_{ij}$ and $H=\tfrac12\sum\|\mathbf V_i\|^2+\sum_{i<j}(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})(1-\dot d_{ij}^2/2)$, the identity $\ddot G=T_{\mathrm{kin}}+H$ was re-derived in Section 11.1. On a bounded sphere history $G$ is bounded and $T_{\mathrm{kin}}+H$ is constant, so $\ddot G=0$ identically; hence $H=-3v^2$, $\sum_{i<j}(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})(1-\dot d_{ij}^2/2)=-6v^2$, $\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}d_{ij}$ is constant and $\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}\ddot d_{ij}=0$ (the PI's conditions). The invariants $\sum\mathbf V_i$, $\sum\mathbf X_i\times\mathbf V_i$ and $H$ follow from the antisymmetry of the pair contributions, their radiality, and the Lagrangian structure.

### 15.2 Per-member conditions on any sphere history (derived)

On a sphere, $d_{ij}^2=2R^2-2\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf X_j$, so $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf e_{ij}=(R^2-\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf X_j)/d_{ij}=d_{ij}/2$: every separation direction makes the same projection $d_{ij}/2$ on the position of either member. Therefore

$$
\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=\tfrac12\sum_{j\ne i}c_{ij}d_{ij}=-v^2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{d_{ij}}\left[1-\frac{\dot d_{ij}^2}{2}+d_{ij}\ddot d_{ij}\right]=-2v^2\qquad\text{for every }i\text{ and }T,
\tag{15.1}
$$

six conditions whose sum over $i$ (each pair counted twice) is the PI's $\sum_{i<j}c_{ij}d_{ij}=-6v^2$. Likewise $\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf e_{ij}=-\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf X_j/d_{ij}$ because $\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf X_i=0$, so the constant-speed condition is

$$
\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{c_{ij}}{d_{ij}}\,\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf X_j=0\qquad\text{for every }i\text{ and }T,
\tag{15.2}
$$

and the separation rate on a sphere is $\dot d_{ij}=-(\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf V_j+\mathbf X_j\cdot\mathbf V_i)/d_{ij}$. Conditions (15.1)–(15.2) are sharper than the summed ones and cost nothing beyond the solve; they are the natural per-member filters for an F3-type search. They are not an obstruction: they are twelve scalar conditions on a state that the law's solve determines, and they are satisfied identically by the hexagon.

### 15.3 All members on great circles (derived reduction, no obstruction)

Suppose every member moves on a great circle, $\mathbf V_i=\Omega\,\mathbf n_i\times\mathbf X_i$ with fixed unit normals $\mathbf n_i$ and $\Omega=v/R$. Then $\mathbf A_i$ lies in the plane of $\mathbf n_i$ (the curve is planar), is orthogonal to $\mathbf V_i$ by (constant speed) and has radial component $-v^2/R$, so $\mathbf A_i=-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ exactly, for every member and time: a great-circle history must be centripetal at the common rate $\Omega$, which is the F2 condition without the antipodal pairing. The implicit solve is then eliminated, because $\ddot d_{ij}=\mathbf e_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf A_i-\mathbf A_j)+\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2/d_{ij}=-\Omega^2d_{ij}+(\|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2-\dot d_{ij}^2)/d_{ij}$ contains no unknown, and the law becomes the closed system

$$
\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{d_{ij}^3}\Big[1-\tfrac32\dot d_{ij}^2+\|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2-\Omega^2d_{ij}^2\Big](\mathbf X_i-\mathbf X_j)=-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i\qquad(i=0,\dots,5),
\tag{15.3}
$$

with $\|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2=2v^2-2\mathbf V_i\cdot\mathbf V_j$. Writing $w_{ij}$ for the bracket divided by $d_{ij}^3$ (symmetric), (15.3) says $L(T)\mathbf X=-\Omega^2\mathbf X$ where $L=\operatorname{diag}(\sum_jw_{ij})-(w_{ij})$ is a Laplacian-type symmetric matrix on the six members and $\mathbf X$ the $6\times3$ matrix of positions: at every instant the coordinate columns of $\mathbf X$ are eigenvectors of $L(T)$ with the same negative eigenvalue $-\Omega^2$, so $-\Omega^2$ has multiplicity at least the dimension of the span of the positions (three for a non-coplanar configuration), while the all-ones vector is in the kernel of $L$ (consistent with $\sum\mathbf X_i=\mathbf 0$). Contracting (15.3) with $\mathbf X_i$ and summing gives $\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}d_{ij}^2=-6\Omega^2R^2=-6v^2$, and subtracting the PI's $\sum_{i<j}(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})(1-\dot d_{ij}^2/2)=-6v^2$ leaves

$$
\sum_{i<j}\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{d_{ij}}\|\mathbf w_{ij,\perp}\|^2=\Omega^2\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}d_{ij},
\tag{15.4}
$$

a constant of the motion on any great-circle history (the right side is constant by Section 15.1). Equation (15.3) is an exact, solve-free evaluator for great-circle candidates: its residual is the matrix-free law residual of the candidate acceleration and vanishes exactly when the solved residual does. No contradiction was found in (15.3)–(15.4) for the general great-circle class; F2 is its antipodal sub-class, excluded on its box by measurement only.

### 15.4 All pairs antipodal (derived reduction, no obstruction)

Suppose the configuration is centrally symmetric with partners of opposite polarity, $\mathbf X_{i'}=-\mathbf X_i$, $q_{i'}=-q_i$. Central inversion combined with the global polarity flip is a symmetry of the law, so the symmetric subspace is invariant and $\mathbf V_{i'}=-\mathbf V_i$, $\mathbf A_{i'}=-\mathbf A_i$ along the history. For the antipodal pair, $d_{ii'}=2R$ is constant, $\dot d_{ii'}=0$, and

$$
\ddot d_{ii'}=\mathbf e_{ii'}\cdot(\mathbf A_i-\mathbf A_{i'})+\frac{\|\mathbf w_{ii',\perp}\|^2}{d_{ii'}}=2\,\hat{\mathbf X}_i\cdot\mathbf A_i+\frac{4v^2}{2R}=-\frac{2v^2}{R}+\frac{2v^2}{R}=0,
\tag{15.5}
$$

using only $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=-v^2$ and $\mathbf V_i\perp\mathbf X_i$. The antipodal partner therefore contributes exactly its static inverse-square acceleration $-K\hat{\mathbf X}_i/(4R^2)$ at every instant, whatever the motion, and the Weber terms of the antipodal pairs are identically invisible on any equal-speed sphere history. The law reduces to: the four non-partner members must supply $\mathbf A_i+K\hat{\mathbf X}_i/(4R^2)$ for each $i$. In the great-circle sub-class (F2) this reads $\sum_{j\notin\{i,i'\}}c_{ij}\mathbf e_{ij}=-(\Omega^2-K/(4R^3))\mathbf X_i$: at the isolated-pair rate $\Omega^2=K/(4R^3)$ the cross-pair accelerations on every member must cancel identically in all three components at all times, and at any other rate they must be exactly radial and of constant magnitude. That is a sharp filter for F2 (its radial, tangential and binormal parts are what Section 7 reports), but no contradiction was derived from it: the two other pairs' contributions to member $i$ are $c_{ij}\mathbf e_{ij}+c_{ij'}\mathbf e_{ij'}$ with $\mathbf e_{ij'}=(\mathbf X_i+\mathbf X_j)/d_{ij'}$, and a proof that these cannot cancel for all $T$ would need control of $c_{ij}$ along the motion, which the implicit $\ddot d_{ij}$ of the cross pairs does not give in closed form.

### 15.5 Mirror symmetry (derived)

If a periodic history is invariant under a reflection $\mathcal M$ through a plane containing the origin, combined with a polarity-preserving permutation of the members, then every member fixed by the permutation lies in the mirror plane at all times (its image is itself), so its motion is planar through the origin: on the sphere it moves on the great circle cut by the plane, and by Section 15.3 its acceleration is exactly $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ with $\Omega=v/R$. Members exchanged in pairs by the permutation are mirror images with the same polarity. For the pattern $(+,+,+,-,-,-)$ the number of fixed members of each sign has the parity of three, so it is one or three: a mirror-symmetric history has, for each sign, either one member on the great circle of the mirror plane and one mirror pair, or all three members on that great circle; in every case at least one member of each polarity moves on the same great circle with acceleration $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$, and (15.3) applies to those members. This is a classification, not an obstruction.

### 15.6 Outcome

No class-level obstruction was found in the time available. What is established: (15.1)–(15.2) as per-member filters on any equal-speed sphere history; the exact centripetal form and the solve-free system (15.3) with the eigenvalue structure $L\mathbf X=-\Omega^2\mathbf X$ and the constant (15.4) on the great-circle class; the identical vanishing of the antipodal Weber terms (15.5) and the resulting cross-pair cancellation condition on the antipodal class; the great-circle consequence of mirror symmetry. Grades: all derived. Open: whether (15.3) admits any non-rigid solution; whether the cross-pair cancellation of Section 15.4 can be excluded analytically; any statement about histories without great circles, antipodes or mirrors beyond (15.1)–(15.2). Falsifiers for the derived statements: a sphere history with $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf A_i=-v^2$ on which (15.1) fails at some member; a great-circle history whose solved acceleration is not $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$; an antipodal equal-speed state with $\ddot d_{ii'}\ne0$ from the solve.

## 18. The great-circle class: Fourier structure, the closest-approach lemma, and where the argument stops (blind, Part 2e, derivations)

Status: derivations only, written blind to the geometry lane's round-3 material. Spot checks of every derived identity on random states: [weber-binding-sphere-reference-F5-identities.mjs](../evidence/weber-binding-sphere-reference-F5-identities.mjs) (run record). The class is that of Sections 15.3 and 17: six members on great circles of the unit sphere ($R=1$), common rate $\Omega$, polarities three and three, each member's acceleration $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$, and the law in the closed form (15.3).

### 18.1 Pair kinematics on great circles

Let $\mathbf m_i=s_i\mathbf n_i$ be the oriented normal of member $i$ (the rotation is right-handed about $\mathbf m_i$). For a pair, write $\gamma_{ij}$ for the angle between $\mathbf m_i$ and $\mathbf m_j$ and put the intersection line of the two planes along $\hat{\mathbf x}$; then $\mathbf X_i=(\cos\phi_i,\sin\phi_i,0)$ and $\mathbf X_j=(\cos\phi_j,\sin\phi_j\cos\gamma,\sin\phi_j\sin\gamma)$ with $\phi_i-\phi_j=\Delta_{ij}$ constant and $\phi_i+\phi_j=2\Omega T+\text{const}$. Hence

$$
\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf X_j=C_{ij}+A_{ij}\cos\tau_{ij},\qquad C_{ij}=\tfrac12(1+\cos\gamma_{ij})\cos\Delta_{ij},\qquad A_{ij}=\tfrac12(1-\cos\gamma_{ij})=\tfrac12(1-\mathbf m_i\cdot\mathbf m_j),\qquad \tau_{ij}=2\Omega T+\alpha_{ij},
\tag{18.1}
$$

so every pair quantity is a function of the single angle $\tau_{ij}$, with frequencies $0$ and $2\Omega$ in $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf X_j$ (not $\Omega$: the product of two first harmonics has only the sum and difference frequencies), and

$$
d_{ij}^2=2(1-C_{ij})-2A_{ij}\cos\tau_{ij},\qquad \dot d_{ij}=\frac{2A_{ij}\Omega\sin\tau_{ij}}{d_{ij}},\qquad \|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2=\Omega^2\big(d_{ij}^2+4A_{ij}\cos\tau_{ij}\big),
\tag{18.2}
$$

and the bracket of (15.3) with the centripetal accelerations inserted,

$$
1-\tfrac32\dot d_{ij}^2+\|\mathbf w_{ij}\|^2-\Omega^2d_{ij}^2=1+4A_{ij}\Omega^2\cos\tau_{ij}-\frac{6A_{ij}^2\Omega^2\sin^2\tau_{ij}}{d_{ij}^2}.
\tag{18.3}
$$

$A_{ij}=0$ exactly when $\mathbf m_i=\mathbf m_j$: the two members co-rotate on one circle at constant separation, and every pair quantity is constant (the rigid case). $A_{ij}>0$ otherwise; $\mathbf m_j=-\mathbf m_i$ (same circle, counter-rotating) gives $A=1$, $C=0$ and $d_{\min}=0$, contact twice per period, so it is excluded, and in general the no-contact condition is $C_{ij}+A_{ij}<1$. All of (18.1)–(18.3) were checked on $200$ random great-circle pairs at five times each to $8\times10^{-14}$. Writing $a_{ij}=2(1-C_{ij})$, $b_{ij}=2A_{ij}$ and $k_{ij}=b_{ij}/a_{ij}\in(0,1)$ for a non-rigid pair, $d_{ij}=\sqrt{a_{ij}}\sqrt{1-k_{ij}\cos\tau_{ij}}$.

**Lemma 18.1 (no non-rigid pair quantity is constant).** For $A_{ij}>0$ the pair weight $w_{ij}d_{ij}^2=(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})[1+4A\Omega^2\cos\tau-6A^2\Omega^2\sin^2\tau/d^2]$ is a non-constant function of $\tau$, and so is $d_{ij}$. *Proof.* If the weight equalled a constant $K$ on an interval, $Kd^3=d^2(1+4A\Omega^2\cos\tau)-6A^2\Omega^2\sin^2\tau$ there; the right side is a polynomial in $c=\cos\tau$ and the left is $K(a-bc)\sqrt{a-bc}$, which is a polynomial in $c$ only if $K=0$; then the right side must vanish identically, and its $c^2$ coefficient is $-2A^2\Omega^2\ne0$. $\square$

### 18.2 Fourier structure of the closed system

Each pair weight $w_{ij}(T)$ is a function of $\tau_{ij}$ alone, hence $\pi/\Omega$-periodic with only even harmonics of $\Omega$, while each position $\mathbf X_j(T)$ is a pure first harmonic. In the member equation $(\sum_jw_{ij}+\Omega^2)\mathbf X_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\mathbf X_j$ both sides therefore carry only odd harmonics, which is consistent, and the equation is an infinite set of conditions (one per odd harmonic and component) on the nineteen parameters. Three scalar consequences carry only even harmonics and must be constant in time: the global $\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}d_{ij}$ (Section 15.1, from $\ddot G=0$), the per-member (15.1), $\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}d_{ij}^2=-2\Omega^2$ for each $i$, and the energy $H$. In each, the rigid pairs ($A=0$) contribute constants and every non-rigid pair contributes a non-constant even-harmonic function (Lemma 18.1), so the non-constant parts must cancel among the non-rigid pairs.

**Corollary 18.2.** In a solution, every member has either no partner on a different oriented circle or at least two. *Proof.* A member with exactly one non-rigid partner has, in its (15.1), one non-constant function plus constants, which cannot be constant. $\square$ Hence the configurations "five members on one oriented circle and one elsewhere" are excluded; all other partitions of six members among circles survive this count.

### 18.3 The closest-approach lemma

The Fourier coefficients of $\sqrt{1-k\cos\tau}$, $0<k<1$, are all strictly negative for $m\ge1$: expanding $(1-k\cos\tau)^{1/2}=\sum_n\binom{1/2}{n}(-k)^n\cos^n\tau$, every term with $n\ge1$ has the sign $(-1)^{n+1}(-1)^n=-1$, and $\cos^n\tau$ expands into $\cos m\tau$ with non-negative coefficients (checked numerically for $k\in\{0.3,0.7,0.95\}$: $c_1,\dots,c_{12}<0$). They decay geometrically: $\sqrt{1-k\cos\tau}$ is analytic in the strip $|\operatorname{Im}\tau|<\cosh^{-1}(1/k)$ with square-root branch points on its boundary, so $c_m\sim K\rho^m m^{-3/2}$ with $\rho=1/k-\sqrt{1/k^2-1}$ and $K\ne0$ (Darboux; the measured ratio $c_{12}/c_{11}$ agrees with $\rho(11/12)^{3/2}$ to three digits at the three $k$). The same branch points govern $1/d$, $\cos\tau/d$ and $\sin^2\tau/d^3$, whose coefficients behave as $\rho^m m^{-1/2}$, $\rho^m m^{-1/2}$ and $\rho^m m^{+1/2}$, the last with the non-vanishing leading constant (at the branch point $\sin^2\tau=1-1/k^2\ne0$). The decay rate of a pair is fixed by $k_{ij}$ alone: the smaller $a_{ij}/b_{ij}=(1-C_{ij})/A_{ij}$, the closer the approach $d_{\min}$ and the slower the decay.

**Theorem 18.3 (closest-approach lemma).** Let a solution of the closed system have at least one non-rigid pair, and let $S$ be the set of non-rigid pairs at which $\kappa_{ij}=(1-C_{ij})/A_{ij}$ attains its minimum over all non-rigid pairs. Group $S$ by the phase $\alpha_{ij}$ modulo $2\pi$. Then in every phase group $\sum\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ij}}=0$. In particular $|S|\ge2$, every phase group of $S$ contains pairs of both polarity products, and a configuration whose closest approach (in the sense of minimal $\kappa$) is attained by a single pair, or by pairs of one polarity product only, or by pairs with pairwise distinct phases, is not a solution. The same conclusion holds for each member separately: for each $i$, let $S_i$ be the set of its non-rigid partners $j$ attaining the minimal $\kappa_{ij}$ among them; then in every phase group of $S_i$, $\sum\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ij}}=0$.

*Proof.* The $m$-th complex Fourier coefficient (in $\tau=2\Omega T$) of the constant $\sum_{i<j}\sigma_{ij}d_{ij}$ vanishes for every $m\ge1$: $\sum_{\text{non-rigid}}\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ij}}\,c_m(k_{ij})\,e^{im\alpha_{ij}}=0$, using $d_{ij}=\sqrt{a_{ij}}\sqrt{1-k_{ij}\cos\tau_{ij}}$. Divide by $c_m(k^\ast)$, $k^\ast$ the common $k$ of $S$ (the largest $k$, hence the largest $\rho$): the terms outside $S$ are $O((\rho'/\rho^\ast)^mm^{3/2})\to0$, so $P_m=\sum_{S}\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ij}}e^{im\alpha_{ij}}\to0$. Grouping by distinct phases $z_l=e^{i\alpha_l}$ with real weights $W_l=\sum_{\text{group}}\sigma\sqrt a$, the Cesàro mean $N^{-1}\sum_{m\le N}P_m\bar z_{l_0}^m\to W_{l_0}$ while it also tends to $0$; so every $W_l=0$. The per-member statement is the same argument applied to (15.1), whose non-rigid terms are $\sigma_{ij}[a_{ij}^{-1/2}F_1+4A\Omega^2a_{ij}^{-1/2}F_2-\tfrac32k_{ij}^2\Omega^2a_{ij}^{1/2}F_3](\tau_{ij})$ with $F_1=(1-k\cos)^{-1/2}$, $F_2=\cos\,(1-k\cos)^{-1/2}$, $F_3=\sin^2(1-k\cos)^{-3/2}$; within $S_i$ the $F_3$ term dominates every other by the factor $m$ and shares the common $k$, so its leading constant divides out and the surviving weights are again $\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{a_{ij}}$. $\square$

**Corollaries.** (a) *F2 (three antipodal pairs on three distinct oriented circles).* For a circle pair with $C\ne0$ the two closest member pairs are the same-polarity ones (if $C>0$) or the mixed ones (if $C<0$), both with the same phase and the same sign, so a solution must have at least two circle pairs attaining the minimal $\kappa$ with opposite signs of $C$, equal $a$ and coincident phases; with $C=0$ the four member pairs of one circle pair split into two phase groups ($\alpha$ and $\alpha+\pi$) each of one sign, so again a second circle pair at the same $\kappa$ and phases is needed. A unique closest circle pair excludes an F2 state, and in the fully symmetric case (three congruent circle pairs, as for the orthogonal triad with equal phase differences) the odd-harmonic conditions require three unit phases with $p_1=p_3=0$, which is impossible ($p_1=0$ forces an equilateral triple, for which $|p_3|=3$; checked numerically). (b) *Three shared oriented circles, members $k$ and $k+3$ co-rotating on circle $k$ (Section 17, stratum B).* Within one circle pair the four cross pairs have phases $\alpha$, $\alpha+\delta_b$, $\alpha+\delta_a$, $\alpha+\delta_a+\delta_b$ with signs $+,-,-,+$, where $\delta_a,\delta_b\ne0$ are the in-circle separations; no phase group inside one circle pair can contain both signs, so the minimal $\kappa$ must be attained on at least two circle pairs with coincident phases and matched $\sqrt a$ weights of opposite sign. (c) *Per member.* For every member, its closest non-rigid partners (minimal $\kappa_{ij}$) must come in phase-matched groups of both polarity products with cancelling $\sqrt{a_{ij}}$ weights; a member whose single closest non-rigid partner is strictly closer than all its others is excluded.

### 18.4 What is reached and where the argument stops

Reached: (iii), a sharp necessary condition (Theorem 18.3, global and per member) with the exclusions of Corollaries 18.2 and 18.3(a)–(c), all derived; a partial (ii): the five-on-one-circle partition, every F2 or shared-circle configuration with a unique closest circle pair, and the fully symmetric F2 triad are excluded. Not reached: (i). The argument stops at configurations in which the minimal $\kappa$ is attained on two or more pairs (or circle pairs) with coincident phases and cancelling weights; for those the leading Fourier order gives no information, the next orders bring in the pairs with the next $\kappa$ together with the subleading terms of the closest ones, and no closed induction was found in the time available. The full odd-harmonic vector equations (the three components of the member equation at frequencies $\Omega,3\Omega,\dots$) were not analysed beyond their parity structure; they are the natural next instrument, since each odd harmonic is a finite linear condition on the Fourier coefficients of the weights, which are explicit functions of $(C_{ij},A_{ij})$ through the coefficients of $(1-k\cos)^{-1/2}$ and $(1-k\cos)^{-3/2}$. The hexagon has no non-rigid pair and is untouched by the theorem, as it must be.
