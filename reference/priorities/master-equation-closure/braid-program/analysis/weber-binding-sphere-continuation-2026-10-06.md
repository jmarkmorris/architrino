## 3. The great-circle class closes on the planar ring

**The class.** Six members move on great circles of one sphere of radius $R$ about its centre. Equal constant speed on paths of equal length forces one common angular rate $\Omega$. Each member $i$ has an oriented normal $\mathbf m_i$ (the axis of its circle, with the sense of travel) and a phase. No two members ever coincide. This class contains the three antipodal pairs on great circles (family F2 of the first run) and six independent great circles (family F5), which the first run could only search numerically.

**Why the equations become explicit.** A member in uniform motion on a great circle has the centripetal acceleration $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ and no other. Once every acceleration is known, the second time derivative of every pair separation is known too, so the implicit acceleration solve of the law disappears. With $d_{ij}$ the separation, $\sigma_{ij}=q_iq_j$ the polarity product and $w_{ij}(T)=\sigma_{ij}Kd_{ij}^{-3}[1-\dot d_{ij}^2/(2c_f^2)+d_{ij}\ddot d_{ij}/c_f^2]$ the scalar weight of pair $(i,j)$ evaluated on the prescribed motion, the law holds exactly when, for every member and every time,

$$
\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}(T)\,\big(\mathbf X_i(T)-\mathbf X_j(T)\big)+\Omega^2\mathbf X_i(T)=\mathbf 0 .
$$

**The idea of the proof.** Two members on the same oriented circle keep a constant separation; call them a rigid pair. Two members on different oriented circles have a separation that oscillates at frequency $2\Omega$: its square is $D_{ij}=2R^2(1-C_{ij})-2R^2A_{ij}\cos(2\Omega T+\alpha_{ij})$ with $A_{ij}=\tfrac12(1-\mathbf m_i\cdot\mathbf m_j)>0$. Such a pair never collides at a real time, but $D_{ij}$ does vanish at complex times, and those complex collision times are simple zeros. The weight $w_{ij}$ contains $D_{ij}^{-5/2}$, so as a function of complex time it has a square-root branch point at each complex collision time of its own pair and nowhere else. The member equation is an identity between analytic functions, so it remains true when time is moved into the complex plane along any path that avoids those points. Carry it once around a complex collision time of one pair: the square root of that pair's $D$ changes sign, the roots of every pair that does not vanish there return unchanged, and the rigid pairs and the centripetal term have no singularity at all. Subtracting the identity before and after the loop leaves only the pairs that vanish at that complex time, and their sum must be zero by itself. Two partners of a member vanish at a common complex time exactly when they vanish at all the same complex times, which happens exactly when their phases $\alpha$ and their closest-to-farthest distance ratios agree; such partners form a coincidence class.

**What the member equation splits into (Lemma 6.3 of the closure document, derived).** For every member, each coincidence class of its partners on other circles sums to zero separately, and the partners on its own oriented circle balance the centripetal term by themselves, as a ring in instantaneous inverse-square balance. This holds for any number of members and any non-zero real polarities.

**Counting.** A class of one partner cannot sum to zero. A class of two would place the member on the straight line through two other points of the sphere, and a line meets a sphere in at most two points. A class of three is excluded by the same collinearity after the two contracted identities are combined (found in review). Two members of one oriented circle are never in the same class of a third member, because their phases differ by their angular separation. A member alone on its circle cannot balance its own centripetal term. So a solution that is not planar needs a class of at least four partners on four different oriented circles other than the member's own, hence five circles with at least two members each, hence at least ten members.

> **Theorem (great-circle closure; Theorem 10.2 of the [closure document](weber-binding-sphere-great-circle-closure.md#10-counting-and-the-main-theorem) with the corrections of its Section 18.1).** Let at most nine members with non-zero real polarities, in particular six members with three of each polarity, move on great circles of one sphere at one common rate without collision, with $K>0$ and $c_f>0$. If the instantaneous Weber comparison law holds for every member at every time, all oriented normals are equal: the members lie on one great circle, turn in one sense, keep constant separations, and satisfy the planar inverse-square ring balance. Conversely every such balanced ring satisfies the law.

Claim grade: derived and independently confirmed for six members. The deriving lane wrote the proof with its own numerical spot checks; a separate reviewer, forbidden the author's scripts, re-derived every lemma, found no gap, and tested the mechanism with its own code: the closed residual against the frozen full-solve library to $2.4\times10^{-14}$, the sign change of the singular root and the return of the others in 30 of 30 random configurations, the growth exponent $-5/2$ to seven digits, and a falsifier search of 700 least-squares starts over the full family and the two-two-two and three-three partitions, which found planar hexagons at residual $\le1.1\times10^{-14}$ and nothing else below $1.0$. Falsifier: a collision-free great-circle state of at most nine members with unequal oriented normals whose closed-system residual over a period, divided by $\Omega^2R$, is below $10^{-8}$ by an independent evaluation. The [review](weber-binding-sphere-great-circle-closure-review.md) requested four corrections of wording or scope and no correction of substance; all are accepted in Section 18.1 of the closure document.

## 4. Uniform circular motions on the sphere

The same method was carried by the deriving lane to a larger class: every member moves uniformly on some circle of the sphere, about its own axis, all at one common speed. This class contains the rigid two-circle arrangements, the stacked latitude circles with unequal rates (where the rate ratios are the inverse radius ratios, the integer-frequency family of the first run), every great-circle family, and circles about different axes with different radii.

**A general formula.** For any prescribed history, with $D=d^2$, the pair weight is $w=\sigma KD^{-5/2}\big[(1+D''/(2c_f^2))D-3D'^2/(8c_f^2)\big]$, and the history satisfies the law exactly when $\sum_jw_{ij}(\mathbf X_i-\mathbf X_j)=\ddot{\mathbf X}_i$ for every member and time. When every position is an entire function of time, as for uniform circular motions, each pair term is $D^{-5/2}$ times an entire vector, and the partners of a member split by whether their $D$ differ by the square of a meromorphic function: each class that is not a perfect square sums to zero by itself, with at least three partners on a sphere, and the perfect-square partners (which include every rigid pair) carry the whole acceleration. This lemma does not apply to an arbitrary true solution of the law, which is real-analytic but need not be entire and whose own complex singularities are not controlled.

**The statement.** > **Uniform-circle theorem (Theorems 16.9, 17.5 and 18.3 of the [closure document](weber-binding-sphere-great-circle-closure.md#16-the-uniform-circle-class-square-classes-mirror-pairs-and-the-six-member-statement); called here the uniform-circle theorem to avoid confusion with Theorem 18.3 of the independent reference).** Let six members with polarities $\pm1$ move uniformly on circles of one sphere, each about its own axis, at one common speed, with no collision at any real time, with $K>0$ and $c_f>0$. If the instantaneous Weber comparison law holds for every member at every time, the history is a rigid rotation: the six angular velocity vectors are equal, the members lie on one latitude circle or on a mirror pair of latitude circles of one axis, every separation is constant, and the law reduces to the rigid inverse-square balance.

The proof has three parts. First, a pair has constant separation exactly when its two angular velocity vectors are equal, and the square of a pair's separation is a perfect square of an entire function, without collision, only for rigid pairs and for mirror pairs (two circles of equal radius related by a reflection plane that the circles do not cross); a mirror partner contributes a double pole in complex time that no other term can cancel, so a solution has none. Second, every other partner lies in a class of at least three, and the perfect-square partners carry the member's whole acceleration, so no member is alone in its rigid group. The count then leaves rigid groups of sizes six, three-and-three, or two-two-two; no rigid group of three balances; groups of two are diametrically opposite unlike pairs of one common radius; and the two members of such a pair can never share a class as seen from a third member. Third, the case left by this argument, a pair on circles of different radii about non-parallel axes whose squared separation might be a perfect square, is excluded for every radius ratio: irrational ratios and ratios with odd sum by degree and coefficient arguments, the ratio $3{:}1$ because its only perfect square is a tangent-circle collision, and the remaining odd ratios by a sparsity argument and a three-term recurrence.

Claim grade, by part. For all circles of equal radius about any axes, and for all axes parallel with any radii and rates, the theorem is derived and independently confirmed: the reviewer re-derived the lemmas, confirmed the formula against the frozen full-solve library to $1.2\times10^{-15}$, the loop mechanism at unequal rates in 30 of 30 configurations, the mirror-pair distance law and double pole on 60 constructed pairs, and ran its own falsifier searches (200 parallel-axis starts with floor $0.59$, 120 equal-radius starts with floor $1.07$, reach cases recovered 20 of 20 and 16 of 16). For unequal radii about non-parallel axes the theorem is derived with one independent reading of every step, including an independent reduction of the $3{:}1$ case to the same triple zero; the reviewer's falsifier search over this case, 333 completed starts with radius ratios pinned at $3{:}1$, $5{:}1$ and $5{:}3$ and with mirror-pair seeds, passed its reach cases (24 of 24 perturbed hexagons recovered after a first design that failed its known case was rejected), ended on rigid hexagons 27 times and found no non-rigid state below $0.23$ in units of $v^2/R$, that floor being a composite of two rigid rings on the lower radius bound of the search. The reviewer would grade the whole statement derived and independently confirmed within stated limits; this synthesis keeps the more cautious wording, derived with one independent reading and a bounded falsifier negative, because two of the lemmas for odd radius ratios have had a single quick independent reading. Five minor remarks of wording were made and no correction of substance. Falsifier: a collision-free non-rigid history of this class whose prescribed-history residual is below $10^{-8}$ by an independent evaluation.

**What this settles about integer frequencies.** Members on parallel circles of different radii at one common speed lap at different rates, in the ratio of the inverse radii. The first run tested that family numerically for rate ratios $1{:}2{:}3$ and $1{:}2{:}2$ and found no balance and no preference for rational ratios. The uniform-circle theorem now excludes every such history, for integer, rational or irrational rate ratios alike, by derivation with independent confirmation. With the first run's rigid results, a six-member equal-speed sphere made of uniform circular motions can only be the alternating hexagon or a rigid two-circle arrangement of the mixed polarity class, and the latter has no balance on the box searched in the first run (measured, adjudicated).

## 5. Multi-curve collocation at $R=1$

**Instrument.** The [collocation lane](weber-binding-sphere-multicurve-collocation.md) represents the six paths as truncated Fourier series with one fundamental frequency and solves the law at equispaced times with the full $18\times18$ acceleration solve at each, without integrating over a lap, so the quarter-lap collisions that left the unconstrained stratum uncovered at $R=1$ in the first run do not arise. Its known cases were recorded before any target and all pass: the hexagon is a zero ($2.7\times10^{-15}$) and is recovered from a 20 % perturbation in six of six runs; and, as a genuinely non-rigid test, the same code with two members reproduces two closed eccentric rosettes of this law (eccentricity $0.3$, apsidal angles $2\pi$ and $3\pi/2$) with period errors $1.5\times10^{-14}$ and $3.2\times10^{-13}$ against the overnight quadrature and fine-grid residuals below $10^{-13}$.

**A defect of the frozen specification, found by the lane.** The preregistration normalized the motion residual by $\omega^2R$. The lane showed, by derivation and in its runs, that this residual tends to zero on non-solutions as $\omega\to\infty$ (members nearly at rest on tiny loops) and to a rate-independent value on fast rigid rotations. The round-1 floors obtained under that normalization are artefacts of the specification and are not cited as negatives. The specification error was the Principal Investigator's; it is corrected in the preregistration, Section 11.6, which normalizes by the squared speed, bounds rate and speed, and requires both normalizations for acceptance.

**Round 1 (measured).** In 370 target starts at three harmonics, across the unconstrained, C2, C3 and D3 strata and four winding classes, no equal-speed sphere candidate other than the hexagon appeared; all 49 reach starts recovered the hexagon. Two periodic orbits off the sphere were found, both rigid rotations (three same-circle opposite pairs on circles of two different radii; and six coplanar members turning about a line in their plane); their members have unequal speeds, so they lie outside the equal-speed target. **Round 2 (measured).** Under the amended specification the lane ran 267 starts at three harmonics (unconstrained and C2, each by the direct route and by periodicity first): 255 targets and 12 reach starts, every reach start recovering the hexagon. No candidate appeared. The amended specification has a weakness of its own, again found by the lane and again the Principal Investigator's: with the speed free, the speed-normalized residual suppresses the inverse-square mismatch like $1/v^2$, so the descent runs to the largest allowed speed, and 250 of 252 non-hexagon results end on the speed bound. Those are box-limited and are not floors. The informative test is the harmonic ladder that the finite-harmonic lemma below calls for: 50 ladders at three, five and seven harmonics, each rung seeded from the last, on the ten best box-limited states, the one interior state, twelve fresh starts, and 27 starts with the speed pinned. Its known cases behave as they should: the hexagon is a zero at every rung, and the non-rigid rosette's residual falls geometrically, by a factor of about $0.38$ per harmonic, from $2.0\times10^{-3}$ at ten harmonics to $1.5\times10^{-14}$ at forty. No six-member ladder does that. Residuals stall, rise or fall slowly at values of order one or larger; the two ladders that fell over three rungs were extended and one then rose again; apart from the hexagon the smallest last-rung residual is $0.67$, on a rigid box-limited state; and all but one of the non-rigid states have a determinant that changes sign or a separation below $0.2R$ at the last rung. In the lemma's terms, the stalls are evidence against a true solution near each of the 47 non-hexagon states laddered, at up to seven harmonics. They say nothing about regions those starts did not reach. Not covered in round 2: winding classes, the C3 and D3 strata under the amended residual, and nine harmonics for most ladders.

**Adjudication of round 1 (independently confirmed).** The derivation lane, using its own curve evaluator and the frozen full-solve reference library and not the collocation library, re-evaluated the round-1 results in the [adjudication](weber-binding-sphere-multicurve-adjudication.md). The hexagon zero and its detuned control are confirmed; both rosettes satisfy the law on an independent solve at 1024 phases (residuals $1.0\times10^{-14}$ and $4.1\times10^{-15}$) with the quadrature periods reproduced to the last printed digit; the two off-sphere periodic orbits are rigid rotations in inverse-square balance whose members have unequal speeds; both degenerate limits are confirmed, the fast-rigid value to eight digits ($8.2594588\times10^{-3}$), and the micro-loop bound with a correction of a factor equal to the number of harmonics; and all 740 target results were re-evaluated, of which 16 meet the candidate tolerances and all 16 are the hexagon. Not adjudicated: the solver internals, the reach reruns and the five-harmonic refinements.

**What a finite number of harmonics can and cannot find.** The adjudication observed that at three harmonics the exact sphere and constant-speed conditions leave little beyond uniformly traversed circles. That observation is now a lemma for every number of harmonics (Lemma 20.1 of the closure document, proposed by the Principal Investigator and checked by the deriving lane; refereed on paper by the independent reviewer and confirmed complete as written, with the remark that it holds equally for finite sums over arbitrary real frequencies): a vector trigonometric polynomial that keeps a constant norm and a constant speed is a uniformly traversed circle. The proof takes the moving frame of the curve, position direction $\mathbf e_1$, unit tangent $\mathbf e_2$ and their cross product $\mathbf e_3$, all trigonometric polynomials, with $\mathbf e_2'+(v/R)\mathbf e_1=k\mathbf e_3$ and $\mathbf e_3'=-k\mathbf e_2$, where the scalar polynomial $k$ is the speed times the geodesic curvature. Degrees add under multiplication, so the second equation gives $\deg\mathbf e_3=\deg k+M$ and the first then gives $2\deg k+M\le M$: the curvature is constant and the curve is a circle. With the uniform-circle theorem this yields a corollary (Corollary 20.2): a collision-free six-member equal-speed spherical history whose positions are finite Fourier sums of one fundamental, and which satisfies the law, is a rigid rotation. So at any finite number of harmonics the exact equal-speed spherical solution set of the collocation instrument contains only rigid rotations. A non-rigid equal-speed spherical solution, if one exists, has infinitely many harmonics and can show in such an instrument only as a residual that falls as harmonics are added; a floor that does not fall is the meaningful numerical signal. The deriving lane adds that the sphere and speed defects are quadratically flat near circles, so the motion residual, not those two, carries the information near a candidate.
