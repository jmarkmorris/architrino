# Rational periodic radius and height cannot supply a nonplanar limiting orbit

## Proposed extension

Claim grade: derived, pending independent reconstruction. Extend the [accepted finite meridional Fourier theorem](overnight2-b-independent-finite-meridional-fourier.md) by allowing radius and height to be rational functions of $w=e^{i\phi}$, real on the unit circle and nonsingular there, with radius strictly positive. A regular periodic solution of the normalized simultaneous limiting equation in this rational class must still be the planar constant-radius circle. The angular phase is unrestricted beyond the limiting angular equation.

The scenario remains the canonical six-member limit with $K=c_f=1$. The complex variable tests exact rational identities only. No physical complex path or imported physical law is used. A finite Fourier approximation with a denominator is therefore not an exact way around the meridional obstruction. Smooth nonrational profiles, including profiles generated by the limiting ODE itself, remain outside this theorem.

## Axial identity with no square-root branches

Let $\phi=\Omega\chi$, $\Omega>0$, and suppose $z$ is not identically zero. Define rational functions
$$
Q_4=r^2+4z^2,\qquad Q_1=r^2+z^2,\qquad
L=-\Omega^2z_{\phi\phi}/z.
$$
The real axial equation, on an arc with $z\ne0$, implies the rational identity
$$
\left(L^2-\frac{16}{Q_4^3}-\frac1{16Q_1^3}\right)^2
=\frac4{Q_4^3Q_1^3}.
$$
This is the independently derived twice-squared identity from the accepted finite-Fourier proof. Its derivation does not require finite polynomial profiles; rationality suffices. Clearing denominators extends the identity from the real arc to an identity of rational functions. Each $Q_j$ is nonzero as a rational function because it is positive on the unit circle.

We use local pole orders at any finite point $w=a\ne0$. A positive order denotes a zero; a negative order denotes a pole. Since $\partial_\phi=iw\partial_w$, a pole of $z$ of order $p\ge1$ gives $z_{\phi\phi}/z$ a pole of order exactly two, with nonzero leading coefficient. The factor $a\ne0$ is essential here. Therefore $L^2$ has pole order four.

## Height has no finite nonzero pole

Suppose $z$ has such a pole. Since $Q_4-Q_1=3z^2$ has a pole, the two radicands cannot both have positive order or both be regular. In particular, at most one can have a zero.

If neither radicand has a zero, both inverse cubes are bounded at $a$. The left side of the rational identity then has pole order eight, while its right side is bounded, an impossibility.

If exactly one radicand has a zero of integer order $k\ge1$, the other has a pole of order $2p$: subtraction of the zero-valued radicand from the identity $Q_4-Q_1=3z^2$ establishes that order exactly. The inverse cube of the zero-valued radicand has pole order $3k$, whereas that of the other has a zero of order $6p$. Inside the square on the left, the singular orders are four and $3k$. They cannot be equal because $3k=4$ has no integer solution, so their leading terms cannot cancel. The left side consequently has pole order $2\max(4,3k)$.

The right side has pole order $3k-6p$ if that number is positive, and is bounded or vanishing otherwise. In every case,
$$
2\max(4,3k)>3k-6p.
$$
The rational identity therefore cannot hold. This exhausts the possible radicand orders and rules out every finite nonzero pole of $z$.

If $z\equiv0$, the same no-height-pole conclusion is immediate and the next radial argument applies directly. A nonzero constant height is already impossible by the real axial equation, but need not be separately excluded for the pole argument.

## Radius has no finite nonzero pole

Now $z$ is regular at every finite nonzero point. Suppose $r$ has a pole of order $p\ge1$ at $w=a\ne0$. Each radicand then has a pole of order $2p$, with leading term equal to that of $r^2$, since $z$ is regular. On a local punctured sector the two square roots have branches whose magnitudes behave as $|r|$; any branch signs leave the following orders unchanged.

The limiting radial equation is
$$
\Omega^2r_{\phi\phi}=\frac{\ell^2}{r^3}+\frac1{\sqrt3r^2}
-\frac{r}{Q_4^{3/2}}-\frac{r}{4Q_1^{3/2}}.
$$
Its left side has a pole of order $p+2$, with nonzero leading coefficient. On the right, the first term vanishes to order at least $3p$ (or is identically zero), the second to order $2p$, and both square-root terms to order $2p$. Thus the whole right side tends to zero while the left is unbounded, a contradiction.

To justify that this local branch equation is the original exact identity, start with the positive square roots on a nonsingular real arc and analytically continue along a path avoiding the finite poles and zeros of the rational functions. Such a path reaches a small punctured sector at $a$. Both square-root germs continue there and the equation remains valid. Its contradiction uses only their magnitudes, so monodromy cannot repair it. No globally single-valued complex square root is assumed.

## Reduction to the accepted finite-Fourier theorem

A rational function with no finite nonzero pole can have poles only at zero and infinity. Multiplication by a sufficiently large power of $w$ removes the possible pole at zero. The resulting rational function has no finite poles, so after cancellation its denominator is constant and it is an ordinary polynomial. Therefore both $r$ and $z$ are Laurent polynomials.

Reality on the unit circle makes them real trigonometric polynomials. The accepted finite meridional Fourier theorem now applies and forces $z=0$, $r=r_0>0$ and $\ell^2=(5/4-1/\sqrt3)r_0$. Thus the only rational-profile limiting orbit is the planar circle. For a compact exact slow sequence whose limiting meridional profiles belong to this rational nonsingular class, positive limiting rotation and the nonzero planar torque mean give the same contradiction as before.

A uniform exclusion over a rational parameter family requires an additional closure hypothesis: subsequences must converge to rational profiles nonsingular on the phase circle, with the usual common radius and $C^2$ bounds. Bounded degrees alone should not silently replace that requirement when denominator degenerations have not been analyzed. A compact coefficient parameterization with a common nonzero denominator floor and normalized coefficients provides one sufficient way to preserve rationality under subsequences, after common-factor cancellation if needed. This document asserts no unrestricted compactness theorem for arbitrary rational representations.

## Falsifiers and preservation

The proposed extension fails if the twice-squared rational identity is wrong, if a pole of $z_{\phi\phi}/z$ at a finite nonzero height pole has an order other than two, if the radicand order classification misses a possible leading cancellation, or if a radius pole with regular height can produce a singular right side in the radial equation. A regular nonplanar limiting orbit with rational radius and height would directly falsify the theorem. Complex branching with noninteger local exponents, nonrational profiles and singular real-phase denominators are outside its hypotheses.

This is an analytical extension without a new numerical target. The prior finite-Fourier subject and independent report remain frozen. No law, production implementation or shared owner was changed. Independent reconstruction and parent integration into the second-allocation report remain before acceptance.
