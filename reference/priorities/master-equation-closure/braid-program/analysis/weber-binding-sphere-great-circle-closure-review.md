## 2. Referee notes, lemma by lemma

**Section 3, the closed system (3.3) and (3.4). Correct.** With $D=d^2$: $d\dot d=\dot D/2$ and $\dot d^2+d\ddot d=\ddot D/2$, so the bracket is $1+\ddot D/2-3\dot D^2/(8D)$. A history of the class satisfies the law exactly when the kinematic acceleration $-\Omega^2\mathbf X_i$ is a fixed point of the right side, which is (3.4). The reviewer derived the relation to the solved form independently: writing the law as $M\mathbf A=\mathbf b$ with $M_{ii}=I-\sum_j(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})\mathbf e\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}$ and $M_{ij}=(\sigma_{ij}/d_{ij})\mathbf e\mathbf e^{\mathsf T}$, the closed residual is $\mathbf F=M\,(\mathbf A_{\rm law}+\Omega^2\mathbf X)$. Test K1 confirms this to $2.4\times10^{-14}$ relative. The closed form is the right condition also where $M$ is singular.

**Section 4, pair kinematics, Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2. Correct.** Re-derived: with both in-plane bases started on the line of nodes, $\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=1-\mathbf m_i\cdot\mathbf m_j$ up to a unit phase, so $A_{ij}=\tfrac12(1-\mathbf m_i\cdot\mathbf m_j)$; $A=0$ exactly when the oriented normals are equal and $A=1$ exactly when they are opposite. For opposite normals $\mathbf b_j$ is a unit multiple of $\bar{\mathbf b}_i$, so $C=0$, $\kappa=1$ and $D=2R^2(1-\cos\tau)$ vanishes at real times: two members on one geometric circle in opposite senses do collide, as claimed (T3: $A=1$, $C=-2\times10^{-16}$, sampled least separation $4\times10^{-6}R$ on a grid of $2\times10^5$ times). Lemma 4.2 is one line, $\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_{j'}=e^{i\delta}\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j$, and is confirmed by T3 without the isotropic-vector formalism. An antipode $-\mathbf X_j(T)$ has $\mathbf b=-\mathbf b_j$, so antipodal partners always share an oriented circle: there is no "antipodal partner on another circle" case to treat.

**Use of the common rate (probe h).** The hypothesis enters at (4.2): only with one rate is $\mathbf X_i\cdot\mathbf X_j$ a constant plus a single cosine of $2\Omega T$. It is then used in everything downstream: the notion of rigid pair (equal normals give constant separation only at equal rates), the lattice (5.1) of complex collision times, and Lemma 6.2. With unequal rates the pair product has the two frequencies $\Omega_i\pm\Omega_j$ and none of this structure survives; Section 12, item 1, of the document says so and the theorem does not claim that case. Equal speed on great circles of one sphere gives $R|\Omega_i|$ equal, and the sense is carried by $\mathbf m_i$, so "equal speed forces one common positive rate" is right. Uniform speed along each circle is part of the class definition (3.2), not a consequence of the law, and the sentence in Section 3 should be read that way (correction C3).

**Section 5, analytic structure. Correct.** $D=2R^2A(\kappa-\cos\tau)$ vanishes only where $\cos\tau=\kappa>1$, never at a real time. There $\sin^2\tau_\ast=1-\kappa^2\ne0$, so $\dot D(T_\ast)^2=16\Omega^2R^4A^2(1-\kappa^2)\ne0$ and every zero is simple (probe b): simplicity is exactly the no-collision hypothesis, and fails only at $\kappa=1$. The value (5.3) of the entire numerator at $T_\ast$ was re-derived and checked (T1: $1.4\times10^{-14}$ relative). The proof that the complex chord $\mathbf N_{ij}$ is not zero is correct (the complexified planes meet in the complex multiples of the real node direction, and $\mathbf Y\cdot\mathbf Y=R^2$ makes the common point real); note that the main line of proof does not need it, since Lemma 6.3 does not use the leading coefficient.

**Lemma 6.2. Complete and correct.** (c)⇒(d) by (4.5); (d)⇒(b)⇒(a) trivially; (a)⇒(c) by comparing imaginary and real parts in (5.1), where positivity of $\operatorname{arccosh}\kappa$ fixes the sign and then $\kappa$, and the real parts give $\alpha$ modulo $2\pi$. Partners with equal $\kappa$ and different $\alpha$ have collision times with the same imaginary part and different real parts, so they are in different classes (T4: at the collision time of one, $|D|/2A$ of the other is at least $0.198$ in 200 constructed cases). T4 also confirms (c)⇒(d) on 200 constructed partners with $A$ ratios from $0.009$ to $304$, to $1.2\times10^{-13}$, at real and complex times.

**Lemma 6.3 (class cancellation). Complete and correct (probes a, b).** Each step was checked. (1) The set $B$ is a finite union of lattices off the real axis, hence closed and discrete, so $\mathbb C\setminus B$ is path connected and a disc about $T_\ast$ free of other points of $B$ exists. (2) Each $D_{ij}$ is entire without zeros off $B$, so the germ of $\sqrt{D_{ij}}$ positive on the real axis continues along every path in $\mathbb C\setminus B$, and with it the whole expression $\mathbf F_i$, as the same algebraic expression in the continued roots. (3) $\mathbf F_i$ vanishes on a real interval, so its germ is the zero germ, and the continuation of the zero germ along any path is zero. This is the standard permanence of functional relations and needs no joint strip of analyticity; the worry that other pairs' roots may have changed sign on the way is immaterial, because whatever sign they carry on arrival, they are holomorphic and single-valued on the whole disc $\Delta$ (their $D$ has no zero there, by Lemma 6.2). (4) The roots of the class members are locked, $s_{ij}=\sqrt{A_{ij}/A_{ij_1}}\,s$, because the relation holds on the real axis between two continuable germs; the lock therefore holds on every sheet, and all of them change sign together around the simple zero. (5) The exponent $-5$ is odd, so the class contribution $s^{-5}\mathbf Q_S$ changes sign and $\mathbf G$ returns; subtracting gives $\mathbf Q_S=\mathbf 0$ near $T_1$, hence identically since $\mathbf Q_S$ is entire with constant coefficients. (6) The return to the real axis is immediate and does not involve the continuation at all: $\mathbf Q_S\equiv\mathbf 0$ is a statement about an entire function, and on the real axis the class sum is $s^{-5}\mathbf Q_S$ with the original positive root. No gap. T1 exercises the mechanics on 30 random states: the singular pair's root returns as $-1$ times itself and the other four as $+1$ in 30 of 30; half the jump of $\mathbf F_i$ around the loop equals the singular pair's term to $1.2\times10^{-15}$; the growth exponent of $\|\mathbf F_i\|$ is $-2.5$ within $10^{-7}$.

**Weights vanishing (probe c).** A pair weight cannot vanish identically: its numerator is a quadratic in $\cos\tau$ with leading coefficient $-2\Omega^2R^4A^2\ne0$. More to the point, nothing downstream divides by a weight. Lemma 8.1 works with identity (I), whose coefficients $\sigma_{ij}A_{ij}^{-1/2}$ are constants, so there are no exceptional times.

**Lemma 7.1. Complete and correct.** The bracket was recomputed: $2R^2Ah+\Omega^2R^4A^2\,[8\cos\tau(\kappa-\cos\tau)-6\sin^2\tau]$ and the last bracket is $g=-6+8\kappa\cos\tau-2\cos^2\tau$. The coefficient of $z^5$ in the class numerator comes only from $g\,\mathbf Y_{1/2}$ and equals $-(\Omega^2R^5/4)e^{2i\alpha_S}\mathbf y_{1/2}$, so (6.1) forces $\mathbf y_{1/2}=\mathbf 0$ and then $h\,\mathbf Y_{3/2}\equiv\mathbf 0$ with $h>0$. This uses $\Omega\ne0$ and a finite $c_f$.

**Lemma 7.2 and Corollary 7.3. Correct; Lemma 7.2 is not used by the main theorem.** The Hermitian-norm step is valid because $|z_\ast|^2=\rho^{\pm1}\ne1$. Corollary 7.3 follows by contracting with $\mathbf b_i$ (bilinear product, $\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_i=0$).

**Lemma 8.1. Complete and correct.** With (I) for a class of two and real non-zero $c,c'$: $c+c'=0$ gives $\mathbf X_j\equiv\mathbf X_{j'}$; otherwise $\mathbf X_i$ is an affine combination of two distinct points of the sphere and lies on the sphere, so it is one of them. Both are collisions. Holds for any non-zero real polarities.

**Reviewer's strengthening of Lemma 8.1 (derived by the reviewer in this session; not yet checked by a second party).** A class of three is also impossible, for any non-zero real polarities. By Corollary 7.3 a class satisfies $\sum_{j\in S}c_j\mathbf X_j(T)=\mathbf 0$ and $\sum_{j\in S}c_j=0$ with $c_j=\sigma_{ij}A_{ij}^{-1/2}\ne0$. For three partners this gives $\mathbf X_{3}=(c_1\mathbf X_1+c_2\mathbf X_2)/(c_1+c_2)$, with $c_1+c_2=-c_3\ne0$: the third partner lies on the straight line through the other two and on the sphere, so it coincides with one of them at every time, a collision. Hence every coincidence class of a solution has at least four partners, on four different oriented circles by Lemma 8.2, none the member's own. A non-planar solution then needs five oriented circles with at least two members each: **the conclusion of Theorem 10.2 holds for every $N\le9$ with any non-zero real polarities**, which makes Lemma 8.3 unnecessary for the count (it still gives the two-and-two polarity structure of a class of four for polarities $\pm1$). The argument uses both identities, (II) for $\sum c_j=0$ and (I) for the vector relation. T5 is consistent with it: over constructed classes of three with free real polarity products, the joint defect of (I) and (II) vanishes only where two partners coincide. Falsifier: a collision-free class of three partners with non-zero real $\sigma_{ij}$ satisfying (I) and (II) to rounding.

**Observation on a class of four (derived by the reviewer; not used by any verdict here; offered for the residual analysis at ten or more members).** With $c_j=\sigma_{ij}A_{ij}^{-1/2}$ and $e_j=\sigma_{ij}A_{ij}^{-3/2}$, a class of four has $\sum c_j\mathbf X_j=\mathbf 0$ with $\sum c_j=0$, so its four partners lie in one plane at every time. Identity (II) reads $\mathbf X_i\sum e_j=\sum e_j\mathbf X_j$. If $\sum e_j\ne0$, the member lies in that same plane, so the member and its four partners, each on a different great circle, would lie on one circle of the sphere at every time. If $\sum e_j=0$, then $\sum e_j\mathbf X_j=\mathbf 0$ is a second affine relation among four points that are not collinear, so $e_j\propto c_j$ and all four $A_{ij}$ are equal. Either alternative is a strong restriction, and T5 found no admissible class of four in its box.

**Lemma 8.2. Complete and correct (probe e).** It is Lemma 4.2. A pair is rigid exactly when $A_{ij}=0$, that is equal oriented normals, so a member of a class is never on the member's own oriented circle, and two members of one oriented circle have $\alpha$ differing by their angular separation $\delta\not\equiv0$ (T3: 200 cases, to $2.1\times10^{-14}$). So "three or more partners on three different oriented circles, none the member's own" follows.

**Lemma 8.3. Correct.** The Cauchy–Schwarz step gives $(\sum x_j)(\sum1/x_j)\ge n^2$ and equality with $1$ forces $n=1$. Reviewed on paper only; superseded for the count by the strengthening above.

**Lemma 9.1. Correct, with a scope correction on (b) (probe f).** (a) follows from (6.2) with an empty sum. (b) as proved requires $\sigma_{ij}=-1$ and gives $\Omega^2=K/(4R^3)$; this is right for polarities $\pm1$. For general non-zero real polarities the same line gives $\sigma_{ij}<0$ and $\Omega^2=|\sigma_{ij}|K/(4R^3)$ (correction C1). Only (a) is used in the count, so Theorems 10.1 and 10.2 are unaffected. Remark 9.2 was checked on paper for the mixed triple (the function $\gamma$ is strictly decreasing and odd about $\pi$) and for three like members (the contraction with $\mathbf X_i$ is positive); it is not used by the main proof.

**The count and Theorem 10.2. Complete and correct (probe g).** The count uses: a non-rigid partner exists if the normals are not all equal; Lemma 6.3 with Lemma 7.1 for that member; Lemma 8.1 (class size); Lemma 8.2 (distinct circles, none the member's own); Lemma 9.1(a) applied to each of the four circles, which needs the law for the members on those circles. Nothing else is used: not the three-and-three split, not the sign of any polarity product, not $N=6$ beyond $N\le7$. The converse is right because every bracket equals $1$ on a rigid ring. The partition cross-check is consistent: of the partitions of six without a part of size one, $\{4,2\}$ falls to Lemma 8.1, $\{3,3\}$ and $\{2,2,2\}$ to the stated arguments, which the reviewer re-derived.

**Degenerate geometries (probe d), summary.** Antipodal partners: same oriented circle, hence both rigid for a member of that circle and in different classes ($\alpha$ shifted by $\pi$) for any other member. Two members on one oriented circle: rigid pair, constant separation $R\sqrt{2(1-\cos\delta)}$, positive exactly when $\delta\not\equiv0$. One geometric circle in opposite senses: $A=1$, $\kappa=1$, collision, outside the class. Coincident normals with different phases: the previous rigid case. Equal $\kappa$, different $\alpha$: different classes. No degenerate case escapes the lemmas.

**Section 11, Theorem 18.3 as a corollary. Confirmed.** For a member, a phase group of its minimal-$\kappa$ partners is one coincidence class, and $\sum\sigma\sqrt A=0$ times $\sqrt{2\kappa_S}$ is the per-member statement. For the global statement, the pairs of a global phase group that contain a given member are exactly one coincidence class of that member (since $\alpha_{ij}=\alpha_{ji}$ and $\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{ji}$), so summing over members counts each pair twice. The F2 and F5 consequences follow from Theorem 10.2 directly.

> Falsifier: a real vector trigonometric polynomial of finite positive degree with constant norm and constant non-zero speed that is not a uniformly traversed circle.

**Referee notes, step by step.**

- *The frame.* $\mathbf e_1=\mathbf X/R$ and $\mathbf e_2=\dot{\mathbf X}/v$ are real vector Laurent polynomials in $z=e^{i\omega T}$ of degree exactly $M$ (the top coefficient of $\mathbf e_2$ is $iM\omega\,\mathbf x_M/v\ne\mathbf 0$, which uses $M\ge1$ and $\omega\ne0$), orthonormal at real times because $\mathbf X\cdot\dot{\mathbf X}=0$; $\mathbf e_3=\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2$ is a real vector Laurent polynomial. I re-derived the three frame equations: $\dot{\mathbf e}_2\cdot\mathbf e_1=-v/R$, $\dot{\mathbf e}_2\cdot\mathbf e_2=0$, and $\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\mathbf e_1\times\dot{\mathbf e}_2=k\,\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_3=-k\,\mathbf e_2$. The expansion of $\dot{\mathbf e}_2$ in the frame is made at real times, where the frame is orthonormal, and the resulting equations then hold as identities between Laurent polynomials because a Laurent polynomial that vanishes on the unit circle is zero. That order of steps is right and is needed: at complex times the frame is not orthonormal in the Hermitian sense.
- *The meaning of degree.* For a non-zero scalar or vector Laurent polynomial the degree is the largest exponent with non-zero coefficient. For a real one the coefficients are conjugate-symmetric, so the exponents are symmetric, the degree is at least $0$, and it is $0$ exactly for constants. This is used three times (for $k$, for $\mathbf e_3$, and to turn "$\deg k\le0$" into "constant") and each use is legitimate because $k$ and $\mathbf e_3$ are real at real times.
- *Can $\deg(k\,\mathbf e_3)=\deg k+\deg\mathbf e_3$ fail?* No. The top coefficient of the product is a non-zero complex number times a non-zero complex vector, which is a non-zero vector. The proof never takes the degree of a dot or cross product of two vector polynomials, where the top coefficient can vanish (for instance $\mathbf x_M\times\mathbf x_M=\mathbf 0$, or $\mathbf x_M\cdot\mathbf x_M=0$, which in fact holds here because $\|\mathbf X\|$ is constant); the degree of $\mathbf e_3$ is obtained from the third frame equation, not from the cross product. This is the point where a careless version of the argument would break, and the written version avoids it.
- *Does $\deg\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\deg\mathbf e_3$ need $\deg\mathbf e_3\ge1$?* Yes, and it is available: the step is taken only in the case "$\mathbf e_3$ not constant", and a real non-constant Laurent polynomial has degree at least $1$; differentiation multiplies the top coefficient by $i\,n\,\omega\ne0$.
- *The count.* If $\mathbf e_3$ is not constant, $k\ne0$ and $\deg\mathbf e_3=\deg k+M$; the right side of the second equation has degree $2\deg k+M$ and equals a left side with no exponent above $M$, so $\deg k\le0$ and $k$ is a constant. (The left side cannot be zero here, since $k\,\mathbf e_3\ne\mathbf 0$.) If $\mathbf e_3$ is constant, $k\,\mathbf e_2=\mathbf 0$ with $\mathbf e_2\ne\mathbf 0$ gives $k=0$. The two cases are exhaustive.
- *From constant $k$ to the circle.* $\mathbf D=k\,\mathbf e_1+(v/R)\mathbf e_3$ has $\dot{\mathbf D}=\mathbf 0$ and $\mathbf D\times\mathbf e_1=(v/R)\mathbf e_2=\dot{\mathbf e}_1$, using $\mathbf e_3\times\mathbf e_1=\mathbf e_2$. So $\dot{\mathbf X}=\mathbf D\times\mathbf X$ with constant $\mathbf D\ne\mathbf 0$: uniform rotation about a fixed axis through the centre, on a circle of radius $v/\|\mathbf D\|>0$, with frequencies $0$ and $\pm\|\mathbf D\|$ only, and the degree $M$ gives $\|\mathbf D\|=M\omega$.
- *Is any case missed?* No. $M\ge1$ and $v>0$ exclude the constant curve; the great circle is the case $k=0$; a circle traversed several times per period is the case $M>1$. The sphere must be centred at the origin of the coordinates, as the lemma states.

**Remark E7 (a generalisation, derived by the reviewer on paper, not needed for Corollary 20.2).** The proof uses only two properties of the exponents: the largest exponent of a scalar times a vector is the sum of the largest exponents, and differentiation does not remove a non-zero largest exponent. Both hold verbatim for finite sums $\sum_\lambda\mathbf x_\lambda e^{i\lambda T}$ over arbitrary real frequencies $\lambda$, with "degree" read as the largest frequency. So the lemma holds for quasi-periodic finite trigonometric sums as well: a finite real trigonometric sum with any frequencies, of constant norm and constant non-zero speed, is a uniformly traversed circle. A corollary for members with incommensurate finite spectra would then need the irrational-ratio case (Corollary 17.3) in addition.
