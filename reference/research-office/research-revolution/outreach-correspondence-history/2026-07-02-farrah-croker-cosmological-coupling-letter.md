# Draft Letter: Cosmological Coupling And Physical Implementation

Status: sent on 2026-07-01, operator-reported; repository copy retained for record.

Primary arXiv page for contact lookup: [arXiv:2302.07878, "Observational evidence for cosmological coupling of black holes and its implications for an astrophysical source of dark energy"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07878)

Dear Professor Farrah, Professor Croker, and collaborators,

I am writing because your cosmological-coupling work appears to do something more important than merely propose another dark-energy parameterization. It turns the black-hole contribution to cosmology into a source-history and boundary-condition problem. That is, it asks whether compact objects normally modeled by isolated or asymptotically simple metrics should instead be treated as finite regions embedded in the actual expanding universe, with mass-energy accounting that cannot be cleanly separated from the cosmological background. From the perspective of Architrino Assembly Architecture ($\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$), that is the most valuable feature of the proposal.

The strongest reading of your result, as I understand it, is not simply "black holes are dark energy." It is that the late-universe energy budget may be missing an implementation layer: a population of compact, finite-boundary regions whose effective cosmological contribution depends on the history of formation, growth, merger, and embedding. In that reading, the interesting object is not a free vacuum-energy substance placed inside black holes. The interesting object is the bookkeeping law that maps compact-object boundary data, cosmic expansion, and population history into an effective stress-energy contribution. That is exactly where the proposal becomes technically important.

The cleanest bridge is the density scaling implied by the coupling index. If the comoving number density is fixed after formation and the coupled black-hole mass scales as $M(a) \propto a^k$, then the effective physical density scales as

$$
\rho_{\rm BH,eff}(a) \propto n_{\rm BH}(a) M(a) \propto a^{-3} a^k = a^{k-3}.
$$

Comparing this with the usual fluid scaling $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w_{\rm eff})}$ gives

$$
w_{\rm eff} = -k/3.
$$

This makes the result unusually transparent. A value near $k=3$ is not just a fit parameter producing a dark-energy-like density. It is the statement that a diluted population of compact regions can maintain an approximately constant effective cosmological density if each member's effective mass contribution grows with the expansion at the compensating rate. In a conventional reading that can sound strange. In an implementation reading it becomes a demand for a precise finite-region energy-accounting law.

Our framework approaches this from a different starting point. We do not begin by assigning an ontological status to vacuum energy in empty spacetime. We ask what physical update rule would make the inherited effective equations true after coarse graining. In that language, your proposal points to a possible recovery target: explain how finite compact regions can carry boundary data, store or release energy, and contribute to the large-scale expansion without being replaced by an isolated stationary idealization. The key issue is not whether the phenomenological coupling can be written down. It is what physical mechanism implements the apparent $M(a)$ law while preserving local conservation, merger accounting, lensing constraints, stellar-remnant history, and high-redshift bounds.

That is also where the proposal seems most vulnerable, and therefore most useful. A skeptic can reasonably ask where the energy comes from, what happens at the boundary, how the accounting avoids double-counting ordinary accretion and mergers, and how the effect survives population uncertainties. In my view, those objections should not be treated as reasons to discard the program. They identify the missing implementation object. A stronger formulation would separate four ingredients: the compact-object internal or boundary state; the local embedding law; the population source term; and the cosmological averaging map. If those are stated independently, then the question becomes testable as a finite-region continuity problem rather than a narrative claim about dark energy.

I also think your work helps clarify a possible mistake in the way dark energy is often discussed. If an observed component behaves like $w \simeq -1$, the default move is to ask what fundamental substance has that equation of state. But the same effective behavior can arise from a source-history ledger: ordinary dilution is offset by a growth, conversion, or boundary contribution. That does not by itself prove the black-hole interpretation. It does show that the equation-of-state label is not automatically the physical implementation. In this sense, your proposal is a useful corrective even if future data revise the preferred coupling value.

The question I would be most interested in seeing made explicit is this: can the cosmological-coupling law be reformulated as a finite-region energy-accounting theorem? More concretely, can one state a local or quasi-local conservation relation in which the effective mass change, the cosmological embedding, the boundary stress-energy, and the population source term are all visible at once? If so, the proposal would become much harder to dismiss as a phenomenological fit. It would become a candidate bridge between compact-object physics and the late-time cosmological energy budget.

For $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$, the importance of your work is that it pressures the usual separation between local compact-object modeling and global cosmological inference. A finite object in an expanding universe should not be forced into a permanently isolated metric if the boundary conditions that define its effective mass are cosmological. Your papers appear to make that point in a form that can be attacked observationally, which is exactly the right standard.

I would welcome any pointer to the most compact technical statement of the coupling mechanism as an energy-accounting law rather than as an effective mass ansatz. That seems to be the decisive step for determining whether the proposal is a physical implementation, an empirical covariance, or a useful false lead that still exposes a real weakness in the inherited modeling pipeline.
