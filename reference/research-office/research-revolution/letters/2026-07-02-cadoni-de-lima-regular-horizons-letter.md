# Draft Letter: Regular Horizons And Cosmological Embedding

Status: sent on 2026-07-01, operator-reported; repository copy retained for record.

Primary arXiv page for contact lookup: [arXiv:2601.03296, "Cosmologically Coupled Black Holes with Regular Horizons"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03296)

Dear Professor Cadoni, Dr. De Lima, Dr. Pitzalis, Dr. Rodrigues, and Dr. Sanna,

I am writing because your construction of cosmologically coupled black holes with regular horizons appears to address one of the most important technical weaknesses in the broader cosmological-coupling discussion. It is one thing to propose an effective relation between compact-object mass and the scale factor. It is another thing to show that the relevant compact object can be embedded in an arbitrary FLRW background while keeping the would-be horizon regular and making the backreaction explicit. From the perspective of Architrino Assembly Architecture ($\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$), that second step is exactly where the physical implementation question becomes sharp.

The most valuable feature of your paper, as I understand it, is not merely the existence of another exact solution. It is the change in standard of proof. A black-hole cosmological-coupling proposal should not be judged only by an isolated stationary metric plus a phenomenological mass law. It should be asked to survive cosmological embedding. That means the horizon, quasi-local mass, stress-energy content, and apparent-horizon structure all have to remain well defined when the background expansion is not treated as an external afterthought.

Your regular-horizon result therefore matters even to readers who are not yet committed to the cosmological-coupling interpretation of dark energy. It identifies a concrete mathematical demand: if compact objects are to carry cosmological boundary data, the geometry must not become singular at the very surface where the local object meets the global embedding. In physical language, a finite region cannot be used as a cosmological source if its boundary description fails under the embedding it is supposed to inhabit.

Our framework reads this as a finite-boundary-data regularity problem. The central question is how much information at the compact-object boundary is enough to continue the object into a cosmological background without generating an unphysical singularity, losing conservation accounting, or hiding energy transfer inside coordinates. Your work seems to supply an important external comparison case: regularity at the relevant horizon can be studied in the same calculation that tracks cosmological expansion and backreaction, rather than being patched in after the fact.

Several distinctions in your setup look especially important for future comparison. The first is the distinction between a static horizon and an apparent horizon. A static chart intuition can make it look as though horizon structure is fixed once the isolated solution is specified. In an embedded cosmological setting, the apparent horizon carries information about expansion, mass content, and causal structure together. That is the right arena for deciding whether a compact object is physically regular in the universe it actually occupies.

The second distinction is the split between local compact-object mass and cosmological mass contribution. A Misner-Sharp-type accounting, or any comparable quasi-local mass accounting, is valuable because it forces the mass question into a geometric ledger rather than leaving it as a single ambiguous parameter. For cosmological coupling, this is not a technical nicety. It is the central issue. One needs to know which part of the mass belongs to the local compact region, which part belongs to the cosmological embedding, and how the two are coupled without double counting.

The third distinction is backreaction. If the embedded object affects the background, and the background affects the object, then the model has moved beyond a test-object picture. That is where the dark-energy relevance becomes more than a metaphor. But it is also where constraints become stricter: energy conditions, perturbations, lensing, formation history, and merger history all become part of the same accounting problem.

For $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$, the broader lesson is that a black-hole model cannot remain only an ontology claim about what sits inside a horizon. It has to become an implementation claim about how finite regions, horizons, boundary data, and cosmological expansion are jointly updated. Your paper appears to advance that standard by showing that regular horizons are not optional decoration; they are part of the viability condition for cosmological coupling.

The constructive question I would most like to see developed is whether the regular-horizon construction can be generalized into a finite-boundary-data theorem. For example: given a compact region with specified quasi-local mass, anisotropic stress structure, and cosmological boundary conditions, what conditions guarantee a regular embedded horizon and controlled backreaction? Such a theorem would be useful even if the final astrophysical interpretation changes, because it would define the mathematical interface between compact-object physics and cosmological source modeling.

A second question concerns dynamical and rotating cases. The observationally relevant population is not made of perfectly static spherical objects. The path from your construction to mergers, spin, accretion, and realistic population synthesis may be difficult, but the conceptual target is clear: preserve the regularity and mass-splitting discipline while relaxing the symmetry assumptions. Even partial results in that direction would help separate robust cosmological-coupling mechanisms from artifacts of a special ansatz.

I would be grateful for any pointer to follow-up work, notes, or preferred derivations that state the regularity conditions in their most invariant form. The connection I see is this: your solution may provide exactly the kind of mathematical bridge needed to turn black-hole cosmological coupling from an effective scaling proposal into a finite-boundary implementation problem.
