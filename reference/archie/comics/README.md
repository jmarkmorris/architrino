# Comics

Comics are compact public entry points for the $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ architecture. Each comic should turn one idea, question, visual contrast, or proof burden into a memorable scene without implying theorem-level closure.

The comics line should protect the same level distinctions used throughout Archie:

- substrate ontology: architrinos, absolute time, Euclidean void, and causal wakes;
- assembly and medium behavior: Noether braids, Noether sea, branch dynamics, and stable patterns;
- effective descriptions: particles, fields, clocks, spacetime, gravity, and quantum behavior as recovered observer-level descriptions;
- validation posture: proof programs, simulations, closure targets, open problems, and public critique.

## Continuity Strategy

Use a stable house style and recurring characters as the default.

The best first continuity system is cross-media reuse:

- Use the video `Educators` for adult, technical, and public-science comics: Mia Quinn for conceptual physics, Sophia Calder for visual mathematics, Julian Brooks for software systems, and Rachel Stone for evidence and skepticism.
- Use Electra and Poz for family or child-facing comics that extend the children's-book line.
- Use anonymous observers, diagrams, source artifacts, app screens, or one-off guest figures only when a one-panel gag, historical contrast, or visual thought experiment works better without the recurring cast.

Random styles and unrelated characters are useful as experiments, but they should not be the default. They can test a platform, meme format, guest-artist direction, or one-off metaphor. Too much randomness makes the project harder to recognize, harder to quality-control, and harder to connect back to the source material.

## House Style

The first style target should be clean, literate, and visually precise rather than chaotic or joke-first.

- Panels should read clearly on a phone.
- Dialogue should be short enough to letter cleanly in speech bubbles.
- Use bubble text as the default reader-facing text: speech bubbles for dialogue, thought bubbles for private realizations, and small caption boxes only when a claim-level note, hidden academic joke, or source-artifact label needs a separate voice.
- Thought bubbles are welcome when the joke depends on a private realization, quiet skepticism, or an academic double-take that should not be spoken aloud.
- Bubble shapes must be rounded or cloud-like. Do not use polygonal, angular, jagged, or boxy speech boxes.
- Attach speech bubbles to the speaker with smooth tapered tails. Attach thought bubbles to the thinker with dot-bubble trails for thoughts, soft uncertainty, or private realization.
- Visual geometry should be part of the scene, not a detached diagram pasted beside the joke.
- Color should help preserve level distinctions: physical objects, causal wakes, Noether sea context, effective diagrams, and source-artifact UI should not blur into one decorative glow.
- The punchline can be funny, but the correction should be accurate.
- When a claim is open, Rachel or the caption should name the proof burden instead of letting the comic imply closure.

## Humor Layers

Each comic should try to work at two humor levels.

The top-level humor should be readable without academic training. It can come from a visible reversal, a character noticing what the audience missed, a too-confident claim being moved to the correct claim level, or a visual mismatch between the familiar observer-level story and the hidden causal geometry.

The academic hidden humor should reward physicists, mathematicians, graduate students, journal readers, open-source contributors, and technically literate skeptics. It can appear in background labels, board notes, paper titles, captions, panel composition, reviewer comments, version numbers, grant language, seminar questions, or the gap between what the panel says and what the academic ritual quietly demands.

Good hidden-humor targets:

- a result becoming `well known` only after everyone stops citing the awkward first version;
- a proof burden being renamed as `future work` in panel `4`;
- a preprint reaching version `v67` because the notation keeps surviving but the claim level does not;
- a seminar question that is actually three objections and a funding history;
- a referee asking for one more comparison model that would double the paper;
- a chart whose most honest axis label is `confidence after coffee`;
- a grant abstract that says `transformative` where Rachel has written `show the residuals first`;
- a whiteboard with `assume closure` crossed out and replaced by `derive closure`;
- a citation needed marker attached to the obvious-sounding sentence.

The hidden humor should be affectionate but sharp. It should make fun of publishing habits, prestige rituals, vague claims, over-polished abstracts, and the social machinery around physics without attacking named real people, journals, institutions, reviewers, or living researchers.

## Satirical Magazine Influence

The useful lesson from classic satirical magazines such as `Mad` is not a need to copy their characters, lettering, mascots, signature devices, or exact visual style. The useful lesson is layered parody: the main joke is readable immediately, while the page rewards rereading with background labels, fake documents, marginal contradictions, absurd institutional names, footnote jokes, and visual side comments.

Use that influence through structure, not imitation:

- crowded but legible background detail;
- fake academic artifacts, such as journal covers, referee reports, conference posters, preprint version notes, grant summaries, and overqualified figure captions;
- deadpan captions that undercut vague prestige language;
- small contradiction jokes in equations, file paths, or whiteboard labels;
- escalation from ordinary misunderstanding to institutional absurdity;
- visual parody of academic ritual, not parody of specific real people or publications.

The recurring characters do not need to become more chaotic to support this. Mia, Sophia, Julian, and Rachel should usually remain the composed center of gravity. Their credibility is what makes the surrounding absurdity work. They can be dry, witty, unimpressed, or visibly precise, but they should not become buffoons, sneering caricatures, or disposable gag figures.

Use a separate satire layer for roles that can absorb sharper parody:

| Satirical role | Function | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Referee Two` | Embodies endless review demands, framework-policing, and comparison-model creep. | Do not imply real reviewers are enemies; the joke is the ritual pressure. |
| `The Grant Abstract` | Turns disciplined uncertainty into inflated institutional language. | Keep the joke on vague language, not on funding science itself. |
| `The Seminar Question` | Starts politely and unfolds into ontology, notation, history, and status anxiety. | Keep the question technically literate, not merely hostile. |
| `The Citation Needed Label` | Appears beside obvious-sounding but unsupported claims. | Use it to teach claim level, not as a lazy dunk. |
| `The Version Number` | Tracks how a paper changes from bold claim to careful proof target. | Let the revision improve the claim rather than only mock revision. |
| `The Prestige Caption` | Says something grand while the panel quietly shows the missing derivation. | Keep the missing derivation visible and specific. |

The house rule is: the cast stays admirable; the institutions, documents, labels, and rituals carry the satire.

## Format Families

Released public gallery comics must use a single-panel, square `1:1` PNG format. The square image is the canonical release asset, not only a thumbnail crop, so the artwork should run to the edge of the square without white or black padding.

### One-Panel Comics

Use one-panel comics for a single contrast, question, or visual punch.

Good one-panel engines:

- `Rachel's check`: a tempting claim appears on a board, and Rachel points to the missing evidence.
- `Hidden history`: a present object is shown with visible causal wakes or path-history behind it.
- `Equation as scene`: Sophia turns one symbol relationship into a geometric arrangement.
- `App artifact`: Julian points to a visible source file, simulation output, or open problem rather than a sales pitch.
- `Mia's mechanism`: Mia names one mechanism in one sentence while the image shows the moving parts.
- `Academic background joke`: the public joke is in the foreground while a board, paper title, caption, or reviewer note carries the hidden academic joke.

### Three- Or Four-Panel Comics

Use three- or four-panel comics as prototype or planning material when the reader needs a small transformation. Do not promote them into the public gallery unless the release format is explicitly changed.

Default structure:

1. Ask: a common question, misunderstanding, or overclaim.
2. Reveal: the hidden level distinction or causal-delay geometry.
3. Test: the skeptic asks what would verify it.
4. Route: the final panel points to the source artifact, simulation, proof burden, or next question.

The fourth panel should usually be the discipline panel, not only the joke panel.

## Main Themes

The strongest recurring themes are:

- the present carries path-history;
- messages and influences take time to arrive;
- stable patterns are not the same as motionless objects;
- the observer-level world is recovered from deeper dynamics;
- records require physical interaction;
- the Noether sea is context, not empty background;
- evidence and proof burdens are part of the story, not an embarrassment;
- open-source work lets readers inspect the source of a claim.

## Academic Spectrum Humor

Use everyday academic frustration as the first joke whenever possible. The reader should be able to recognize the scene before recognizing the physics layer.

Good foreground engines across the academic spectrum:

- freshman: `Will this be on the exam?`, unread syllabus, office hours, unit confusion, and the first collision with a hidden assumption;
- bachelor's student: problem-set panic, group projects where one person does all the work, lab-report cleanup, and notation that changes names halfway through;
- doctoral student: advisor comments that begin with `quick`, proof sections that move the problem instead of solving it, and the discovery that a simple assumption was carrying the argument;
- postdoc: too many tabs, temporary funding, benchmark churn, and turning one clean idea into six deliverables;
- professor: grant language, committee load, referee demands, conference questions, and the art of saying `interesting` while reaching for the residuals.

Shared-academic-pain jokes should also include fear of funding cuts, fear of general change, and the institutional anxiety around a `new theory`. These work best when everyone in the room has a different version of the same worry: the freshman asks whether the exam changed, the doctoral student sees a chapter rewrite, the postdoc sees a funding risk, and the professor sees a committee meeting. The $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ layer should enter as the disciplined `new theory` being tested, not as a demand that the reader already agree with it.

The $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ layer should usually be the second read: explicit assumptions, path-history, proof burdens, parsimony, causal delay, recovery targets, and open-source inspection. If the reader needs to know publishing culture or $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ terminology before the joke works, the panel is too esoteric.

## Arc Architecture

Start with short arcs that can produce both one-panel and three- or four-panel versions.

| Arc | Core question | Main cast | Typical endpoint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hidden Delay | Why does the present depend on earlier source positions? | Mia, Sophia, Rachel | A reader can explain causal-delay intuition without treating it as ordinary instantaneous force. |
| Records And Measurement | What makes a measurement a record? | Rachel, Mia, Sophia | A reader sees measurement as physical interaction and record formation, not magic observation. |
| Geometry You Can See | What is the equation doing geometrically? | Sophia, Mia | A reader can map a symbol or closure target to a visual relation. |
| Public Workbench | How do I inspect the claim? | Julian, Rachel | A reader is routed to source files, simulations, or open proof burdens. |
| Effective World | Why do clocks, rulers, particles, and spacetime appear? | Mia, Sophia, Rachel | A reader can distinguish substrate ontology from recovered observer-level descriptions. |
| Failure Modes | What would make this wrong or incomplete? | Rachel plus any character | A reader learns that falsifiers and closure targets are part of serious theory work. |

## First Prototype Recommendation

Begin with a small repeatable set rather than a broad random anthology:

1. `Signals From Earlier`: one-panel public joke plus visible causal-delay geometry.
2. `Group Project Assumption`: one-panel everyday academic frustration plus explicit-assumption discipline.
3. `Future Work, Obviously`: one-panel public joke about the `do later` box plus proof-burden discipline.
4. `Rachel Checks The Claim`: one-panel credibility comics.
5. `Julian Opens The Artifact`: four-panel open-source route comics.

After `10` prototypes, review which format produces the clearest reader questions, strongest sharing, and cleanest route back to the source material. Keep random or unrelated styles as a controlled experiment lane, not the main identity.

## Going-Forward Specification

Future adult/academic comic batches should use shared academic pain as the normal entry layer before the $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ layer appears.

Required recurring foreground pressures:

- fear of funding cuts or grant instability;
- fear of general change, including syllabus, lab, dissertation, benchmark, committee, and institutional changes;
- anxiety around a `new theory`, especially when different career stages hear different consequences before hearing the claims.

The `new theory` joke should not ask the reader to accept $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ in advance. The scene should first make the human reaction legible: the freshman worries about the exam, the bachelor's student worries about the lab report, the doctoral student worries about a chapter rewrite, the postdoc worries about funding or benchmarks, and the professor worries about committees, grants, and review politics.

The second read should then route the reaction into disciplined theory testing: what changes, what predicts, what breaks, what survives, what evidence exists, and what proof burden remains. Fear of change is allowed to be funny, but it is not a falsifier.

## Production Notes

- Keep scripts, dialogue, captions, prompts, source references, and final images traceable.
- Released public gallery comics must be single-panel square `1:1` PNGs, and each released comic must have a square thumbnail derived from the square release asset.
- Keep dense text editable outside generated image assets until the final lettering pass.
- Do not imitate real people, celebrities, public figures, professors, influencers, or existing fictional characters.
- Avoid jokes that depend on personal-life details of recurring characters.
- Avoid implying that generated characters are real researchers or real people.
- Every released comic should have a clear claim level: established explanation, working hypothesis, proof target, open problem, app/tutorial instruction, or playful analogy.
