# Story Design

This document defines what the children's book series should teach, how the concepts should unfold by age, and what story framing to avoid.

## Core Aim

The series should teach children to see nature as lawful, active, and geometrical:

- there are two fundamental entity types;
- entities transmit and receive influence;
- emitted potential waves travel at a finite field speed;
- reception changes action, force, and acceleration;
- path is fundamental from the beginning: presence here, intended motion there, and return through remembered places;
- path-history later turns lived path into physical bookkeeping;
- assemblies form when interactions close into stable patterns;
- self-action becomes possible when an entity or assembly meets its own causal history.

The stories should be fun first: play, experiments, motion, puzzles, chase games, light, rhythm, building, rolling, balancing, and discovery. The physics should be embedded in action, not delivered as a lecture.

## Two-Level Story Architecture

Each age band should be tracked at two levels:

**Level 1: Lessons.** This is the compact teaching promise for the book or age band. It should contain only one to three lessons. It answers: what do we want the child to carry after the story?

**Level 2: Scenes.** This is the detailed spread-by-spread plan. Each scene should include read-aloud text, image direction, the relevant $\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}\mathbb{A}$ geometry, and any quiet background concept for later learning. It answers: what does the child see and hear on this page?

Level 2 scenes should not introduce new teaching goals casually. Each scene should serve one of the Level 1 lessons for that age band.

## Framing To Avoid

Avoid social overtones. The series should not use the physics palette, polarity, named architrinos, or assembly behavior as metaphors for human groups, personality types, social identity, conflict, morality, or belonging.

Do not map:

- red/blue polarity to good/bad;
- red/blue polarity to gender, temperament, culture, nationality, or ability;
- attraction/repulsion to friendship, rejection, liking, disliking, belonging, or exclusion;
- neutral balance to social compromise;
- assembly to community or family morals.

Characters may be representative of children worldwide in informal play, but the story meaning remains physical: motion, potential, transmission, reception, force, acceleration, path-history, and assembly.

## Response-Type Metaphors

Avoid `positive`, `negative`, `attraction`, `repulsion`, `like`, `unlike`, `opposite`, `friend`, `enemy`, `belong`, and `reject` in child-facing story text. These words import too much moral, social, or arithmetic baggage.

Prefer metaphors based on neutral physical response:

| Avoid | Prefer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| positive / negative | blue kind / red kind | Names the two entity types without value judgment. |
| charge | polarity / kind / response type | Keeps the child-facing concept tied to behavior rather than electric-charge baggage. |
| attract / repel | close-turn / far-turn | Describes the path change instead of a social relation. |
| pull / push | inward answer / outward answer | Names the direction of response without personifying it. |
| like / unlike | same-kind / cross-kind | Describes pairing without social approval. |
| opposite | other-kind | Avoids conflict framing. |
| stuck together | held pattern | Emphasizes assembly geometry, not attachment emotion. |

Best child-facing metaphor:

> Red and blue are two ways a tiny sender-receiver can answer. One answer bends the path inward. One answer bends the path outward.

For ages 3-8, use motion language:

- bends inward;
- bends outward;
- curves inward;
- curves outward;
- path opens;
- path closes;
- the route changes.

For ages 9-11, introduce `response type`:

> Electra and Poz have different response types. Their waves do not mean good or bad. They tell the next path how to bend.

For ages 12+, connect the metaphor back to formal language:

> The child-facing inward/outward response maps to the sign of the interaction term in the force law. The story avoids social language, but the mathematical content remains directional acceleration along the line of action.

## Directional Vocabulary Modalities

Children can handle strong vocabulary when the word is taught through motion, image, and repeated use. The series should use plain direction words first, then introduce etymology-backed challenge words that name the same geometry more precisely.

Primary ladder:

| Rank | Term pair | Literal teaching hook | Best age/use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | toward / away | one path aims nearer; one path carries farther | all ages, read-aloud language |
| 2 | nearer / farther | distance gets smaller; distance gets bigger | baby through age 8 |
| 3 | converge / diverge | from Latin roots meaning bend or tend together / bend or tend apart | ages 6+ as challenge vocabulary |
| 4 | inward / outward | motion relative to a center, wake, ring, or assembly | diagrams and geometry overlays |
| 5 | approach / recede | come nearer / move back or withdraw in distance | ages 9+ and parent notes |
| 6 | contract / expand | draw together / spread out | waves, fields, and assemblies |
| 7 | concentrate / disperse | gather toward a center / scatter apart | many-point scenes and Noether sea imagery |
| 8 | confluent / diffluent | flow together / flow apart | older students, poetic back matter, water analogies |
| 9 | centripetal / centrifugal | toward-center / away-from-center tendency | ages 12+, with care because "seeking" and "fleeing" roots can sound agentive |

The flagship challenge pair should be `converge` / `diverge`. These words are valuable because their roots already carry the right nonverbal geometry: bending or tending together, bending or tending apart.

Read-aloud bridge:

> The paths converged. They bent toward the same place.
>
> Then the paths diverged. They widened away.

Mathematical bridge for older readers:

> Toward means the separation gets smaller. Away means the separation gets bigger. Converging paths narrow. Diverging paths widen.

For a point at position $x(t)$ relative to a source position $s$, the child-facing idea becomes the older mathematical idea that the distance $r(t)=\|x(t)-s\|$ is decreasing, increasing, or momentarily balanced.

## Named Architrinos

Named architrinos can help children remember the two fundamental entity types, but they should be introduced only after the child understands send/receive behavior.

Recommended timing:

- ages 3-5: no named architrinos. Use "tiny dots," "tiny senders," "red kind," and "blue kind."
- ages 6-8: optional first names at the end or in a side activity, not as the main story engine.
- ages 9-11: introduce named architrinos as recurring demonstration characters.
- ages 12+: use the names only as mnemonic aids while moving toward canonical terms.

Working names:

| Story name | Color | Canonical role | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electra | Blue | One architrino polarity type | Child-facing mnemonic for polarity B. |
| Poz | Red | Opposite architrino polarity type | Child-facing mnemonic for polarity A. |

Use `Electra` and `Poz` as named examples, not as the official ontology. The canonical term remains `architrino`, with intrinsic polarity. The names should never imply personality, moral role, gender role, or social identity. They are handles for two fundamental entity types.

Preferred introduction sentence for ages 9-11:

> In our story, we call one blue architrino Electra and one red architrino Poz. The names help us follow them, but what matters is what they do: each keeps a path, sends potential waves, and receives waves from others.

## Age-Band Story Curriculum

### Baby-Toddler Foundation: Here, There, Back

This stage is not a separate technical book unless the series later needs a board-book prequel. It is the developmental foundation for all later books.

What to teach:

- I am here;
- I can go there;
- I can turn around;
- I can come back to an earlier place;
- motion has a felt route.

Avoid:

- named architrinos;
- red/blue polarity as explanation;
- attraction/repulsion language;
- causal-wake terminology;
- any social metaphor.

Story engine:

A baby or toddler crawls, toddles, reaches, rolls a soft ball away, follows it, and returns to the blanket. The story is physical orientation: here, there, gone, back, again.

Key sentence:

> Here, there, back again.

AAA geometry in art:

- a simple dotted path behind the child or ball;
- very faint red/blue marks only as nonverbal path rhythm, not named polarity;
- no formal arrows unless they are part of a game or toy.

### Ages 3-5: Nature Remembers Motion

What to teach:

- path as a visible trail;
- motion leaves traces;
- before and after matter;
- near and far feel different;
- repeated motion can make a pattern.

Avoid:

- named architrinos;
- force equations;
- explicit field speed;
- attraction/repulsion language;
- social metaphor.

Story engine:

A child follows rolling balls, claps, ripples, shadows, and echoes. The child walks a path, returns to an earlier place, and sees that motion leaves a trail. The repeated line can be:

> It moved, and the world remembered.

AAA geometry in art:

- clear paths and return paths before any abstract diagram;
- red and blue dots as simple "two tiny kinds";
- pale red/blue rings from earlier positions;
- purple where rings overlap;
- white neutral centers only in very simple balanced patterns.

### Ages 6-8: The Message That Traveled

What to teach:

- path as route from source to receiver;
- transmission and reception;
- causal delay;
- distance and direction;
- line of action;
- first stable assembly.

Avoid:

- making named architrinos central;
- heavy terminology;
- attraction/repulsion language.

Story engine:

Children play signal games: claps, bells, puddles, strings, marbles, and a bead mobile. They discover that messages travel and that the place where a message started matters.

Key sentence:

> A message can be real before it reaches you.

AAA geometry in art:

- visible source-to-receiver paths;
- source-position dots;
- expanding causal wake arcs;
- one clean line of action when a wake reaches a receiver;
- first hint of red/blue pair balance in a simple bead assembly.

### Ages 9-11: The Tiny Transceivers

What to teach:

- named architrino examples: Electra and Poz;
- two fundamental entity types;
- path as the identity-preserving route of a transceiver;
- polarity;
- potential;
- potential wave emission;
- reception;
- action/force/acceleration;
- superposition;
- assembly.

Avoid:

- treating Electra and Poz as personalities;
- using red/blue as moral or social symbols;
- attraction/repulsion language in child-facing pages;
- using "charge" as the default child-facing term before polarity is clear.

Story engine:

The children build a tabletop "message lab." Electra and Poz appear as trackable red and blue point transceivers in a simulation layer above the real experiment. Each named point keeps a path, emits potential waves, receives waves, and changes motion.

Key sentence:

> Electra and Poz are not heroes or teams. They are two tiny kinds of sender-receiver.

AAA geometry in art:

- Electra and Poz as red/blue luminous points;
- potential waves as emitted arcs;
- force arrows along the line of action;
- acceleration shown by a changed path after reception;
- purple superposition regions where multiple waves meet.

### Ages 12-14: The Balance Point

What to teach:

- potential landscape;
- path as route through a potential landscape;
- action and acceleration near thresholds;
- deterministic multistability;
- settling basin;
- measurement interaction;
- Noether sea as quiet physical medium.

Avoid:

- "random magic" framing;
- social compromise metaphors for neutral balance;
- claiming proof closure beyond the lesson.

Story engine:

A balance experiment and simulation show that tiny changes can send the same system into different basins. A flashlight measurement nudges the system. The children learn that uncertainty in prediction does not mean lawlessness.

Key sentence:

> The rule did not disappear just because the path was sensitive.

AAA geometry in art:

- red/blue basin paths;
- purple threshold region;
- white neutral point at balance;
- Noether sea specks as quiet context;
- measurement beam as physical interaction.

### Ages 15-16: The History That Pushes Now

What to teach:

- path-history as required state;
- distinction between the visible path and admissible causal history;
- field speed;
- admissible causal history;
- self-action;
- self-hit;
- causal-root ledger;
- first master-equation intuition.

Avoid:

- pretending the exact equation is simple;
- using named architrinos as substitutes for variables;
- overloading story pages with formal notation.

Story engine:

Students run a delayed-interaction simulator. A moving red or blue point can receive waves from earlier positions, and in special regimes an entity can meet its own old emission. The story becomes a mystery: which past messages can reach now?

Key sentence:

> To know the motion now, you must know which past waves can arrive now.

AAA geometry in art:

- path-history trails;
- finite-speed wave fronts;
- accepted and non-accepted past intersections;
- self-action loops;
- ledger-like panels in back matter only.

### Ages 17-18: The World We Recover

What to teach:

- assembly hierarchy;
- Noether sea response;
- effective clocks and rulers;
- photon channel;
- effective metric;
- mass-map and recovery targets at a high level;
- claim levels: observed, simulated, derived, open.

Avoid:

- presenting open targets as settled;
- letting analogy replace derivation;
- social or spiritual framing.

Story engine:

Older students use models to ask how familiar physics can be recovered from architrino entities, potential waves, field speed, assemblies, and Noether sea response. The story becomes a proof-and-simulation adventure.

Key sentence:

> The familiar world is not the starting point here. It is what the architecture must recover.

AAA geometry in art:

- nested assemblies;
- photon-channel motifs;
- clock/ruler response diagrams;
- effective metric as observer-level overlay;
- claim-level tags in back matter, not in child-facing art.

## Story Rules

Every story should have:

- one physical question;
- one playful experiment or adventure;
- one visual geometry lesson;
- one child-facing sentence worth remembering;
- one adult note mapping the story to canonical terms.

Every story should avoid:

- moralizing the physics;
- social allegory;
- hidden curriculum about identity or behavior;
- unexplained adult jargon in read-aloud text;
- in-image labels or equations.

## Canonical Term Discipline

Use canonical terms in parent/teacher notes and older age bands:

- architrino;
- polarity;
- transmission;
- reception;
- potential;
- potential wave;
- field speed;
- action;
- force;
- acceleration;
- path-history;
- causal wake;
- line of action;
- assembly;
- self-action;
- self-hit;
- Noether sea.

Use child-facing phrases first:

- tiny kind;
- sender-receiver;
- traveling message;
- old path;
- path-bend;
- speed of the message;
- held-together pattern;
- quiet helpers.

When the child-facing phrase is introduced, preserve the mapping. Do not invent fresh synonyms in every book.
